A Note From the Author
Almost every day in my rheumatology practice, a patient asks me, 
"What should I eat?" It is one of the best questions anyone can ask. 
Medicines have changed what is possible for people with rheumatoid 
arthritis, gout, lupus, osteoarthritis and many other conditions. But 
what is on your plate, three or more times a day, every day, is also 
part of your treatment.
That is why the theme of this component is nutrition is medicine. 
Food will not replace the medicines your doctor prescribes, and no 
single "miracle food" cures arthritis. But a steady pattern of real, 
whole foods can help control weight, protect the heart, support 
bones and muscles, lower the risk of gout attacks, and help your 
medicines do their job.
 Rheumy, the RheumNotes© chatbot 
guide, can help patients find foods, 
read labels and plan simple meals.
This white paper explains nutrition in plain language. It covers how food affects inflammation, the 
difference between macronutrients and micronutrients, why it helps to limit refined carbohydrates, and 
the new federal food pyramid released in January 2026. It also includes photo tables of common foods 
with their calories and nutrients, drawn from the U.S. Department of Agriculture's FoodData Central 
database, so readers can compare foods side by side.
This content is written for patients, families and caregivers, and it is meant to be used by any 
rheumatology or medical practice. It is general by design and is never a substitute for advice from your 
own doctor or a registered dietitian, especially if you have kidney disease, diabetes or food allergies, or 
take medicines that interact with food.
Small changes add up. I hope this helps you take the next step.
Frederick T. Murphy, DO, FACP, FACR
Rheumatologist, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania

Key Takeaways
Ten things to remember
1. Food is part of treatment. It works alongside your medicines, not instead of them.
2. Build meals around real, whole foods: vegetables, fruits, protein foods, dairy, healthy fats and whole 
grains.
3. A Mediterranean-style eating pattern has the best evidence in arthritis care. The American College of 
Rheumatology (ACR) suggests it for people with rheumatoid arthritis.
4. Weight matters for joints. For knee and hip osteoarthritis, losing extra weight is one of the most 
strongly recommended treatments.
5. Limit highly processed foods, added sugars, sugary drinks and refined carbohydrates such as white 
bread, chips and pastries.
6. Eat fish rich in omega-3 fats, such as salmon, sardines and trout, about twice a week if you can.
7. Gout: limit alcohol (especially beer), organ meats, some seafood, and drinks sweetened with high-
fructose corn syrup.
8. Protect your bones, especially if you take steroids such as prednisone: get enough calcium and vitamin 
D.
9. If you take methotrexate, take the folic acid your doctor prescribes.
10. Ask before you supplement. Some vitamins, herbs and supplements interact with arthritis medicines. 
Talk with your doctor or a registered dietitian.

Nutrition Is Medicine
When we think of medicine, we usually think of pills, shots or infusions. But the food you eat every day 
sends signals to your whole body. It affects your weight, blood sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol, gut 
bacteria, bones, muscles and the level of inflammation (the body's defense response, which can cause 
swelling, heat and pain when it stays switched on).
What food can and cannot do
Food CAN help Food CANNOT
Support a healthy weight, which takes stress off knees, 
hips and feet
Lower the risk of heart disease, which is higher in many 
types of inflammatory arthritis
Keep bones and muscles strong
Lower uric acid and the risk of gout attacks
Help some medicines work better, with fewer side effects
Replace disease-modifying medicines for rheumatoid 
arthritis, lupus or other autoimmune diseases
Cure arthritis
Undo joint damage that has already happened
Work overnight. Benefits come from a steady pattern 
over months and years
Why it matters in rheumatic disease
 Heart health: People with rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, psoriatic arthritis and gout have a higher risk 
of heart disease. In the large PREDIMED trial, people at high heart risk who ate a Mediterranean diet 
with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts had about 30% fewer heart attacks, strokes and heart-related 
deaths than people on a lower-fat control diet.
 Weight and joints: Extra body weight adds force across the knees, hips and feet with every step. In 
the IDEA trial, adults with knee osteoarthritis who lost about 10% of their weight with diet plus 
exercise had less pain and better function than those who only exercised.
 Gout: Diet, alcohol and weight all affect uric acid, the substance that forms gout crystals.
 Medicines: Steroids can raise blood sugar and weaken bones. Methotrexate blocks folate. Some 
medicines interact with alcohol or grapefruit.
Nutrition is medicine
Think of each meal as a small dose. One meal will not make or break your health, but the pattern of 
meals you eat over months and years does make a difference.

How Nutrition Affects Inflammation
Inflammation is the body's way of fighting infection and healing injuries. In autoimmune diseases such as 
rheumatoid arthritis and lupus, the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, and 
inflammation does not turn off the way it should. Food does not cause or cure these diseases, but 
research shows that eating patterns can nudge inflammation up or down.

Research reviews have linked diets high in refined starches and sugar with higher levels of C-reactive 
protein (CRP), a blood test doctors use to measure inflammation. Diets rich in vegetables, fruits, whole 
grains and omega-3 fats are linked with lower levels.
The Mediterranean diet
The Mediterranean diet is a way of eating based on the traditional foods of countries around the 
Mediterranean Sea. It is a pattern, not a strict plan:
 Plenty of vegetables, fruits, beans, nuts and whole grains
 Extra-virgin olive oil as the main fat
 Fish and seafood at least twice a week
 Moderate amounts of poultry, eggs, cheese and yogurt
 Less red meat and fewer sweets
 Herbs and spices in place of some of the salt
In a small Swedish study, people with rheumatoid arthritis who followed a Mediterranean diet for 3 
months had less disease activity and better physical function. The 2022 ACR guideline for rheumatoid 
arthritis conditionally recommends a Mediterranean-style diet. "Conditional" means the benefit is likely 
but the evidence is limited, so the choice should be made together with your care team. The same 
guideline does not recommend other special diets or dietary supplements for rheumatoid arthritis.
Omega-3 fats
Omega-3 fats are healthy fats found mostly in fatty fish (EPA and DHA) and in walnuts, flaxseed and chia 
seeds (ALA). The body uses omega-3s to make substances that help calm inflammation. In an Australian

trial of people with early rheumatoid arthritis, those who took high-dose fish oil along with standard 
medicines were more likely to reach remission and less likely to need extra medicines. Fish oil 
supplements can thin the blood and interact with some medicines, so ask your doctor first. Eating fish is 
a safe first step for most people.
Ultra-processed foods and sugar
Ultra-processed foods are made mostly from refined ingredients and additives. Examples are packaged 
snacks, sugary cereals, soda and many frozen meals. In a careful National Institutes of Health study, 
adults ate about 500 more calories a day and gained weight on an ultra-processed diet compared with a 
minimally processed diet, even though the meals offered were matched for sugar, fat, fiber and salt. The 
new Dietary Guidelines for Americans advise avoiding sugar-sweetened drinks and say one meal should 
contain no more than 10 grams of added sugars.
Weight and your joints
Body fat is not just stored energy. It releases chemicals that promote inflammation. Extra weight also 
adds force on weight-bearing joints. In the Framingham Study, women who lost about 11 pounds (2 
body mass index units) had about half the odds of developing knee osteoarthritis. The 2019 
ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline strongly recommends weight loss for people with knee or hip 
osteoarthritis who are overweight.
Gout and purines
Gout is a painful arthritis caused by crystals of uric acid in the joints. Purines are natural substances in 
food (and in the body) that break down into uric acid. Large studies found that men who ate the most 
meat and seafood had a higher risk of gout, while men who ate more dairy had a lower risk. Purine-rich 
vegetables did not raise the risk. Beer, liquor and sugar-sweetened soft drinks also raised the risk. See 
the Low-Purine section later in this paper.

Macronutrients and Micronutrients
Every food is made of nutrients, and nutrients come in two main groups. Macronutrients ("macro" 
means large) are the nutrients we need in large amounts, measured in grams. They give us calories, 
which is energy. Micronutrients ("micro" means small) are vitamins and minerals. We need them in tiny 
amounts, measured in milligrams (mg) or micrograms (mcg), but they are just as important.

The Importance of Limiting Carbohydrates
Carbohydrates are not "bad." Vegetables, fruits, beans and whole grains are all carbohydrate foods, and 
they carry fiber, vitamins and minerals. The problem for most Americans is too many refined 
carbohydrates and added sugars. The new Dietary Guidelines for Americans say to "significantly 
reduce" highly processed, refined carbohydrates such as white bread, packaged breakfast foods, flour 
tortillas and crackers.

Glycemic index and glycemic load
The glycemic index (GI) tells how quickly a carbohydrate food raises blood sugar compared with pure 
glucose (sugar). The glycemic load (GL) goes one step further: it also counts how much carbohydrate is 
in a normal serving. A food can have a high GI but a low GL if a serving holds only a little carbohydrate; 
watermelon is a common example. A large U.S. study of women found that those whose diets had the 
highest glycemic load had higher CRP, a marker of inflammation.
 Choose whole, minimally processed carbohydrate foods most of the time.
 Pair carbohydrates with protein, healthy fat or fiber (for example, an apple with peanut butter).
 Watch portion sizes of rice, pasta, bread and potatoes.
 Drink water or unsweetened drinks instead of soda, sweet tea or juice drinks.
Steroids and blood sugar
Glucocorticoids (steroids such as prednisone and methylprednisolone) are common in rheumatology. 
They can raise blood sugar, even in people who do not have diabetes. A review of 13 studies found that 
about 1 in 3 people without diabetes who took steroids developed high blood sugar, and about 1 in 5 
developed diabetes. If you take steroids, limiting sugary drinks, sweets and refined starches is especially 
important. Ask your doctor whether you need blood sugar checks.

A balanced approach
The new guidelines note that some people with chronic diseases may do better on a lower-
carbohydrate diet, and they advise working with your health care professional to choose the right plan. 
Very low-carbohydrate diets are not right for everyone. They can be hard to follow, may be low in fiber, 
and may require medicine changes for people with diabetes. A sensible goal for most people is to cut 
back on sugar and refined starches while keeping vegetables, fruits, beans and whole grains.
Simple swaps
White bread → 100% whole-wheat bread  ·  Soda → sparkling water with lemon  ·  Sugary cereal → 
oatmeal with berries  ·  Chips → a small handful of nuts  ·  White rice → brown rice or beans

How the new pyramid fits arthritis care
 Good news for most people with arthritis: more vegetables, fruits, protein and healthy fats and 
fewer processed foods support weight control, heart health and muscle strength.
 Protein supports muscle, which protects joints. The guidelines note that older adults still need 
plenty of protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D and calcium.
 If you have gout, the focus on protein does not mean eating lots of red meat, organ meats or 
shellfish. Choose dairy, eggs, moderate portions of poultry, beans, lentils and tofu more often. See 
the Low-Purine section.
 If you have heart disease or high cholesterol, ask your doctor how full-fat dairy and saturated fat fit 
your plan. The guidelines still cap saturated fat at 10% of calories.
 If you have kidney disease (for example, lupus nephritis), you may need less protein, salt or 
potassium. Work with your doctor or a dietitian.
 For chronic disease, the guidelines say some people may do better on a lower-carbohydrate diet 
and should choose a plan with their health care professional.

Bioflavonoids
Bioflavonoids (also called flavonoids) are natural plant compounds that give many fruits, vegetables, 
teas and cocoa their bright colors. They are not vitamins or minerals, but they work alongside them. In 
laboratory studies, flavonoids act as antioxidants (they protect cells from damage) and can calm 
inflammation. In large population studies, people who eat more flavonoid-rich foods tend to have lower 
rates of heart disease.
Most evidence in people comes from observational studies, which show links but cannot prove cause. 
Flavonoid supplements have not been proven to treat arthritis, and some can interact with medicines. 
The best approach is to eat a variety of colorful plant foods.

Low-Purine Eating for Gout
Purines are natural compounds in foods. When the body breaks them down, it makes uric acid. Too 
much uric acid can form needle-shaped crystals in the joints and cause gout attacks. Diet is only one part 
of the picture. Genes, kidney function, weight and some medicines (such as water pills) also matter, and 
most people with gout need a urate-lowering medicine such as allopurinol.
What the 2020 ACR gout guideline says
 Limit alcohol, whatever the type (conditional recommendation).
 Limit purine intake, especially from organ meats and some seafood (conditional).
 Limit high-fructose corn syrup in sodas and sweetened foods (conditional).
 Lose weight if you are overweight (conditional).
 Do not take vitamin C supplements for gout (conditional recommendation against).
 The guideline notes that diet changes alone usually lower uric acid only a small amount, so they are 
added to medicine, not used in place of it.
What else research shows
 Beer and liquor raised gout risk the most in a large study of men; moderate wine did not raise risk in 
that study. Beer also contains purines from yeast.
 Sugary sodas and fructose raised gout risk. Fruit juice and very sweet fruits contain fructose, so 
keep portions small.
 Dairy (milk and yogurt) was linked with a lower risk of gout.
 Purine-rich vegetables such as spinach, cauliflower, mushrooms and peas did not raise gout risk.
 The DASH diet (rich in vegetables, fruits and low-fat dairy) lowered uric acid in a clinical trial, more 
so in people whose levels started high.
 Cherries: in a study of 633 people with gout, eating cherries over a 2-day period was linked with a 
35% lower risk of an attack. This was an observational study and the ACR makes no formal 
recommendation about cherries, but cherries are a healthy fruit choice.
How to read the purine tables
Purine values are total purines in milligrams per 100 grams of food, measured by Kaneko and colleagues 
(2014). Their groups are: very low (under 50), low (50–100), moderate (100–200), high (200–300) and 
very high (over 300).
The values come from food samples tested in Japan, so U.S. foods may differ. The name in parentheses is 
the food that was tested. Nutrition values are from USDA FoodData Central for the serving shown.

Alcohol, arthritis and your medicines
Alcohol can raise uric acid and trigger gout attacks, especially beer and liquor.

Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 
2025–2030 (USDA/HHS)
Eat real, whole foods; limit highly processed foods, added sugars and 
refined carbohydrates.
Protein at every meal (1.2–1.6 g/kg/day); dairy 3, vegetables 3, fruits 2 and 
whole grains 2–4 servings a day (2,000-calorie pattern).
Saturated fat under 10% of calories; sodium under 2,300 mg a day; drink

Before making big changes to your diet, starting supplements or following a special diet, talk with your
