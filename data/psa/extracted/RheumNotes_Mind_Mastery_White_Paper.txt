A Note From the Author
Patients often tell me, "Doctor, every time life gets hard, my joints get 
worse." They are not imagining it. The brain and the body are in 
constant conversation. Worry, grief, money troubles and long days of 
caregiving all send signals through nerves and hormones that can turn 
up pain, disturb sleep and stir up inflammation.
The good news is that the conversation runs both ways. A calmer 
mind can quiet the body. That is the heart of this component: a calm 
mind is medicine. Like any medicine, it works best when it is taken 
every day, in the right dose, and with patience.
Rheumy, the RheumNotes© chatbot 
guide, can lead short breathing breaks 
and daily stress check-ins.
This white paper explains, in plain language, how stress works in the body and how it affects arthritis, 
the immune system and pain. It then walks through more than 20 non-drug ways to strengthen the 
mind, from meditation and prayer to breathing, Tai Chi, journaling, music, time in nature and laughter, 
along with what the research says about each one. It ends with a practical, step-by-step pathway and 
simple daily practices to quiet the mind.
People come to this topic from many faiths and from no faith at all. Every practice here is offered with 
respect, and you are free to choose the ones that fit your beliefs and your life.
This content is written for patients, families and caregivers, and it is meant to be used by any 
rheumatology or medical practice. It is general by design. It does not replace the medicines and care 
your own doctor provides; it works alongside them.
Mastery of the mind is not about never feeling stress. It is about learning to meet stress with skill, one 
breath and one day at a time.
Frederick T. Murphy, DO, FACP, FACR
Rheumatologist, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania

Key Takeaways
Twelve things to remember
1. Mind and body are one system. Thoughts and feelings travel through nerves and hormones to the 
joints, muscles, heart and immune system, and the body sends signals back.
2. Some stress is normal and even helpful. Short bursts of stress help us meet a challenge. Trouble 
comes when the stress alarm stays on for weeks or months.
3. Chronic stress can fuel inflammation. Long-term stress is linked with higher levels of inflammatory 
messengers such as IL-6, slower wound healing and weaker vaccine responses.
4. Stress can worsen arthritis. In studies, daily stress has been followed by more symptoms in 
rheumatoid arthritis and by flares in lupus. Stress is a common trigger for psoriasis and fibromyalgia 
flares.
5. Stress turns up the volume on pain. Worry, poor sleep and low mood make the nervous system more 
sensitive, so the same joint can hurt more.
6. The mind can be trained. Like a muscle, the mind gets stronger with regular practice. Resilience is a 
skill, not a fixed trait.
7. Many non-drug tools work. Mindfulness, CBT, relaxation, Tai Chi, yoga, exercise, music, nature, 
writing, gratitude, social connection and prayer all have research support.
8. Quiet the mind every day. Start with just 5 minutes of meditation, prayer or slow breathing. Build to 
15–20 minutes over 8 weeks.
9. Slow your breathing. Breathing slowly, about 6 breaths a minute with a long exhale, is one of the 
fastest ways to calm the stress response.
10. Move, connect and rest. Gentle movement, time with people you care about and good sleep are 
powerful stress medicines.
11. Have a plan for stress spikes. Know your early warning signs and your first three steps before a hard 
day arrives.
12. Ask for help. Talk to your doctor or a mental health professional if stress, worry or sadness lasts more 
than two weeks. In a crisis, call or text 988, or call 911.

A Calm Mind Is Medicine: Why Strengthening the Mind 
Matters
Most of us were taught to think of the mind and the body as two separate things. Science now shows 
they are deeply connected. The brain controls the nerves, hormones and immune cells that reach every 
joint. In turn, pain, inflammation and poor sleep send signals back to the brain that shape our mood and 
thoughts. Doctors call this the mind–body connection.
Figure 2. A few quiet minutes a day can calm the body's stress response. Photo: Amy Serin, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia 
Commons.
Strengthening the mind
Strengthening the mind does not mean forcing yourself to "think positive" or pretending that pain is not 
real. It means building skills that help you notice what is happening inside you, calm your body on 
purpose, see problems clearly, and choose how you respond. These skills can be learned at any age, and 
they get stronger with practice, just like muscles.
This idea is very old. Nearly 2,000 years ago, the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote in his private 
journal:
“Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for 
the soul is dyed by the thoughts.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book V.16 (George Long translation)
Resilience: bending without breaking
Resilience is the ability to bend under stress and then bounce back. It does not mean that hard things do 
not hurt. Resilient people still feel sadness, worry and anger. They also have ways to recover: people to

when a flare, a bad test result or a family problem arrives. This paper gives you a simple daily plan and a 
step-by-step pathway to do exactly that.

How Stress Works in the Body
The word "stress" in medicine was made famous by Hans Selye, a Hungarian-Canadian doctor often 
called the father of stress research. In 1936 he described how animals exposed to many different strains 
(cold, injury, toxins or overwork) all showed the same basic body response. Later he defined stress 
simply:
“Stress is the nonspecific response of the body to any demand made upon it.”
— Hans Selye, Stress Without Distress (1974)
In other words, stress is the body getting ready to meet a demand. The demand can be good, like a 
wedding or a new grandchild, or hard, like pain, illness or loss. Selye also pointed out that "complete 
freedom from stress is death." Stress is part of being alive. The goal is not to get rid of it, but to keep it in 
a healthy range and to recover well afterward.
The stress alarm: two lanes
When your brain senses a threat or a worry, it sounds an alarm. The alarm travels down two lanes at 
once:
 The fast lane (nerves). The sympathetic nervous system signals the adrenal glands, which sit on top 
of the kidneys, to release adrenaline (also called epinephrine). This happens within seconds.
 The slower lane (hormones). This lane is called the HPA axis, short for hypothalamus–pituitary–
adrenal. The hypothalamus in the brain sends a hormone called CRH to the pituitary gland. The 
pituitary releases ACTH into the blood. ACTH tells the adrenal glands to make cortisol, the body's 
main stress hormone. This takes minutes and lasts for hours.

Fight-or-flight
Together, adrenaline and cortisol create the fight-or-flight response. It was designed to help our 
ancestors escape danger. Your heart pumps faster, breathing quickens, muscles tense, blood sugar rises 
for quick energy, and digestion and sleep are put on hold. This is exactly what you want if you need to 
jump out of the way of a car.
The opposite state is sometimes called rest-and-digest. It is run by the parasympathetic nervous 
system, including the long vagus nerve. In this state, heart rate slows, breathing deepens, muscles 
loosen, digestion resumes and the body repairs itself. Harvard cardiologist Herbert Benson called the 
ability to switch on this calm state on purpose the relaxation response. Many of the practices in this 
paper work by turning it on.

Brain scientist Bruce McEwen called this wear and tear allostatic load. When stress is long-lasting, or 
when the body cannot shut the alarm off, the same hormones that protect us in the short term begin to 
cause harm. Researchers have also found that chronic stress can make immune cells less sensitive to 
cortisol, which normally helps calm inflammation. As a result, inflammation can run higher than it 
should.
Why this matters for arthritis
Cortisol is the body's own anti-inflammatory hormone; prednisone is a man-made version of it. When 
stress is short, cortisol helps control inflammation. When stress is chronic, the system gets worn out and 
inflammation can rise. Calming the stress response every day helps keep this system in balance.

How Stress Affects Arthritis
Many people with arthritis notice that a flare follows a stressful stretch of life. Research supports what 
patients have long reported, although stress is only one of many things that affect disease activity. Stress 
does not "cause" arthritis by itself, and a flare is never your fault. But managing stress is one more tool 
you can use.
What the research shows
Condition Stress and the disease
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) In a Dutch study that followed 80 people with RA for six months, daily stress predicted 
more fatigue the next month, and worrying predicted more pain, more swollen joints 
and higher disease activity the next month.
About 1 in 6 people with RA has major depression, which can make pain and disability 
worse.
Lupus (SLE) In a German study that followed 41 women with lupus for six months, daily stress, 
especially stress in relationships, was linked with flares.
A large U.S. study of women found that those with post-traumatic stress disorder 
(PTSD) had nearly three times the risk of later developing lupus.
Psoriasis and psoriatic 
arthritis (PsA)
Stress is one of the most commonly reported triggers of psoriasis flares, and psoriasis 
and psoriatic arthritis share the same inflammatory pathways.
Living with visible skin disease can itself be stressful, creating a cycle.
Fibromyalgia Fibromyalgia is closely tied to how the nervous system processes pain and stress.
Physical or emotional stress can trigger or worsen symptoms. Mind–body treatments 
such as exercise, Tai Chi and CBT are core parts of care.
Osteoarthritis and back pain Stress does not wear down cartilage, but it tightens muscles, worsens sleep and lowers 
pain tolerance, which can make the same joint hurt more.
Stress may also play a role before disease starts. A large Swedish study of more than 100,000 people 
found that those diagnosed with stress-related disorders, such as PTSD, had about a 30% to 40% higher 
risk of later developing an autoimmune disease than people without these disorders. U.S. research has 
also linked more childhood adversity with a higher chance of being hospitalized for an autoimmune 
disease as an adult. These studies show links, not proof that stress causes the disease, but they point in 
the same direction.
Stress and flares: a practical view
Keep track of stressful events along with your symptoms in the Mind Practice Tracker at the end of this 
paper. Over time you may see patterns that help you and your doctor plan ahead. Never stop or change 
your arthritis medicines because you feel calmer or more stressed; talk with your doctor first.

How Stress Affects the Immune System
The immune system protects us from germs and repairs injured tissue. It is also the system that goes off 
course in autoimmune arthritis, such as RA, lupus and psoriatic arthritis. The immune system "listens" to 
stress hormones, so long-term stress can change how it works.
Inflammation: IL-6 and CRP
Immune cells talk to each other using chemical messengers called cytokines. Some cytokines, such as 
interleukin-6 (IL-6), turn inflammation up. IL-6 tells the liver to make C-reactive protein (CRP), a blood 
test your doctor may check to measure inflammation. Several arthritis medicines work by blocking IL-6 
or similar messengers.
Chronic stress can push these messengers higher. In a six-year study, older adults caring for a spouse 
with dementia had increases in IL-6 about four times as fast as similar adults who were not caregivers. 
Other research shows that chronic stress makes immune cells less responsive to cortisol's calming signal, 
so inflammation is harder to turn off.

How Stress Affects Pain
Pain is real. It is also always processed by the brain. The nerves carry signals from a joint to the spinal 
cord and brain, and the brain decides how loud to make them. Stress, worry, fear, poor sleep and low 
mood can all turn the volume up. Calm, rest and a sense of control can turn it down.
Central sensitization: when the volume stays high
When pain lasts a long time, the nervous system can become more sensitive. Doctors call this central 
sensitization. The pain "amplifier" in the spinal cord and brain gets stuck on high, so normal touch or 
movement can hurt and pain spreads beyond the original joint. Central sensitization plays a large role in 
fibromyalgia, and it can also add to pain in osteoarthritis, RA, lupus and back pain. Stress is one of the 
things that keeps the amplifier turned up.
The stress–pain–sleep–mood cycle
Stress, pain, sleep and mood feed one another. Stress tightens muscles and raises pain. Pain breaks up 
sleep. Poor sleep lowers the pain threshold and darkens mood the next day. Low mood makes it harder 
to move, connect with others and cope, which adds more stress. The good news is that you can break 
the cycle at any point.

Family 1: Quieting practices
Mindfulness meditation and MBSR
Mindfulness means paying attention, on purpose, to the 
present moment, without judging it. You might focus on your 
breath, the feeling of your feet on the floor, or sounds around 
you. When the mind wanders (and it will), you gently bring it 
back. In 1979, Jon Kabat-Zinn created Mindfulness-Based Stress 
Reduction (MBSR) at the University of Massachusetts Medical 
School. It is an 8-week group course with weekly classes, daily 
home practice, gentle yoga and a body scan.
What the research shows: In Kabat-Zinn's first study of 51 
people with chronic pain who had not improved with usual 
care, 65% had at least a one-third drop in pain after 10 weeks. A 
review of 47 trials found that meditation programs improve 
anxiety, depression and pain. In a trial of 342 adults with 
chronic back pain, MBSR and CBT both helped more people 
than usual care. In people with RA, a mindfulness program 
helped most in those with a history of depression. And in 2023, 
an 8-week MBSR course worked as well as a common anti-
anxiety medicine for adults with anxiety disorders.

Breathing: diaphragmatic, slow and box breathing
Breathing is the one part of the stress response you can control 
directly. Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing means letting the

of breathing high in the chest. Slow breathing means about 5 to 
6 breaths a minute, with a longer exhale than inhale. Box 
breathing means breathing in, holding, breathing out and 
holding, each for a count of 4.

of a healthy stress response) and improves relaxation and 
mood. In a 2023 Stanford study, 5 minutes a day of breathing 
practice for one month improved mood, and "cyclic sighing"

Progressive muscle relaxation
Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) was developed by American physician Edmund Jacobson in the 
1920s and 1930s. You gently tense one muscle group for about 5 seconds, then release it for 10 to 20 
seconds and notice the difference, moving from the feet up to the face. Over time, you learn to spot and 
release hidden tension.
What the research shows: In a head-to-head study, 20 minutes of PMR, deep breathing or guided 
imagery each increased relaxation compared with a control group. PMR is part of many pain-coping 
programs for arthritis and fibromyalgia.
Try it: Tense only gently (about half strength) and skip any painful or inflamed joint. Just notice and relax 
that area instead.
Guided imagery
Guided imagery uses your imagination to create a calm scene, such as a beach, a garden or a favorite 
place, using all five senses. A recording, an app or a therapist can guide you, or you can guide yourself.
What the research shows: A review of 7 randomized trials in adults with arthritis and other rheumatic 
diseases found that guided imagery, usually by audio recording, improved pain and other outcomes in 
every study, with low risk of side effects.
Try it: Close your eyes and picture a place where you feel safe and at ease. What do you see, hear, smell 
and feel? Stay there for 5 minutes.

Biofeedback
Biofeedback uses sensors to show you, in real time, what your 
body is doing, such as muscle tension, heart rate, breathing or 
skin temperature. Seeing the numbers change helps you learn 
to relax on purpose. Heart rate variability (HRV) biofeedback 
teaches slow, steady breathing that balances the nervous 
system.
What the research shows: A review of 24 studies found that 
HRV biofeedback was linked with a large drop in stress and 
anxiety. A review of 21 studies in chronic back pain found that 
biofeedback reduced pain, disability, depression and muscle 
tension, with benefits lasting an average of 8 months.
Figure 10. A biofeedback training session. Photo: Army 
Medicine, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.
Try it: Ask your doctor or a physical therapist or psychologist about biofeedback. Some wearable devices 
and apps offer guided HRV breathing.
Family 2: Thinking skills
Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and pain-focused CBT
CBT is a short-term, practical form of talk therapy. It teaches 
you to notice unhelpful thoughts (such as "This pain will never 
get better"), test them, and replace them with more balanced 
ones, and to change behaviors that keep you stuck. CBT for 
chronic pain adds skills such as pacing, relaxation, problem-
solving and sleep habits. It can be done in person, in groups, by 
video or through some online programs.
What the research shows: A Cochrane review of 75 trials and 
more than 9,000 adults with chronic pain found that CBT leads 
to small but real improvements in pain, disability and distress. A 
review of 27 trials in rheumatoid arthritis found that 
psychological treatments improve pain, disability, depression, 
anxiety and physical activity, especially when they include goal 
setting and self-monitoring. In an RA trial, pain-focused CBT 
gave the biggest improvements in pain control and lowered the 
inflammatory messenger IL-6 in lab tests.
Figure 11. A counseling session. Photo: Healme24, CC0, 
via Wikimedia Commons.
Try it: Practice the "catch it, check it, change it" habit. Catch an upsetting thought, check whether it is 
fully true, and change it to a fairer one. Ask your doctor for a referral to a psychologist trained in CBT for 
pain.
Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)
ACT (said like the word "act") helps people stop fighting with pain and difficult feelings, and instead 
make room for them while still doing what matters most. It combines mindfulness with a focus on 
personal values: being a good grandparent, gardening, serving your community.

Journaling and expressive writing
In the 1980s, psychologist James Pennebaker asked people to 
write about their deepest thoughts and feelings about a 
stressful or upsetting event for 15 to 20 minutes a day, for 3 or 
4 days. This is called expressive writing. Everyday journaling, 
such as writing down worries, plans or good moments, is a 
gentler form.
What the research shows: In Pennebaker's early study, 
students who wrote about upsetting experiences made fewer 
visits to the health center in the following months. In a 1999 
trial in JAMA, people with rheumatoid arthritis who wrote 
about the most stressful event of their lives had improvement 
in disease activity at four months, while those who wrote about 
neutral topics did not.

Try it: Write for 15 minutes about something that weighs on you. Do not worry about spelling or 
grammar. If writing about a trauma becomes too upsetting, stop and talk with a counselor.
Gratitude
Gratitude means noticing and appreciating the good things in life, large and small: a phone call from a 
friend, a pain-free hour, a warm cup of tea.
What the research shows: In a well-known study, people who wrote down five things they were grateful 
for each week felt better about their lives as a whole, were more optimistic about the coming week, 
reported fewer physical complaints and spent more time exercising than people who listed hassles.
Try it: Each evening, write down three good things from the day and why they happened. This also 
works well as a bedtime routine.

Family 4: Connecting and enjoying
Social connection and support groups
Humans are wired for connection. Talking with someone who 
listens, laughing with friends, belonging to a faith community or 
joining an arthritis support group all calm the stress system.
What the research shows: A review of 148 studies and more 
than 300,000 people found that those with stronger social 
relationships had a 50% greater chance of survival over the 
study periods, an effect comparable to well-known risk factors 
such as smoking. In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General warned that 
a lack of social connection may raise the risk of early death

Nature and time outdoors
Time in green spaces such as parks, gardens, woods or by the 
water, or even sitting near a window with a view of trees, helps 
the mind settle.
What the research shows: In a study of nearly 20,000 people in 
England, those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature 
were more likely to report good health and well-being. It did 
not matter whether the time came in one long visit or several 
short ones. In another study, a 90-minute walk in nature (but 
not in a city) reduced rumination, the repetitive negative 
thinking linked with depression, and calmed a brain area tied to 
it.

Try it: Aim for 2 hours a week outdoors, spread out any way you like. Morning light also helps sleep.
Music
Listening to calming music, singing, humming or playing an 
instrument can shift mood quickly.
What the research shows: A review of 104 randomized trials 
with nearly 10,000 people found that music lowered stress, 
with benefits for heart rate, blood pressure and stress 
hormones as well as for feelings of stress and anxiety.

Laughter relaxes muscles, brings people together and gives a 
break from worry.
What the research shows: In six experiments, people who 
laughed together while watching comedy had higher pain 
thresholds afterward than those who watched neutral videos, 
likely because laughter releases the body's natural painkillers, 
called endorphins.

Family 5: Daily habits and tools
Sleep
Stress and poor sleep feed each other. A racing mind keeps you awake, and a short night makes stress, 
pain and mood worse the next day.
What the research shows: A review of 72 studies found that sleep disturbance is linked with higher 
levels of inflammation markers such as CRP and IL-6. Poor sleep and pain are closely tied in both 
directions.
Try it: Keep a steady bedtime and wake time, and end the day with 10 minutes of a quieting practice. 
See the RheumNotes© Sleep/Restore white paper for full guidance.
Limiting news and screen time
Constant news, social media and alerts keep the brain on alert. 
Scrolling late at night also delays sleep.
What the research shows: After the 2013 Boston Marathon 
bombings, people across the U.S. who watched 6 or more hours 
a day of news about the attack had more acute stress than 
people who were directly exposed to the bombings. In a trial of 
college students, limiting social media to 30 minutes a day for 
three weeks reduced loneliness and depression.
 Figure 19. Endless scrolling keeps the stress alarm 
switched on. Photo: Japanexperterna.se, CC BY-SA 2.0, 
via Wikimedia Commons.
Try it: Check the news once or twice a day at set times, not right before bed. Turn off non-essential 
phone alerts and try one screen-free hour each evening.
Setting goals and pacing
Stress grows when there is too much to do and too little energy. Goal setting breaks big tasks into small, 
doable steps. Pacing means planning activity in manageable chunks with rest breaks, so you avoid the 
"boom-and-bust" pattern of overdoing it on good days and paying for it on bad days.
What the research shows: Goal setting, planning and self-monitoring are the parts of psychological 
programs for RA that are linked with the biggest benefits for mood and anxiety. People who took an 
arthritis self-management course, which teaches goal setting, pacing and problem-solving, still had 
about 20% less pain and 40% fewer doctor visits four years later.
Try it: Each week, make one small "action plan" that is specific and realistic: what, how much, when and 
how often. Rate your confidence from 0 to 10. If it is below 7, make the plan smaller.

Step 4: Have a plan for flares and stress spikes
Hard days will come. A plan made on a calm day is much easier to follow on a hard one. Fill in this plan 
and keep it on your refrigerator or phone.
My stress-spike and flare plan: S.T.O.P.
S – Stop. Pause what you are doing. Notice your stress number and where you feel it in your body.
T – Take a breath. Do 5 to 10 slow breaths: in for 4, out for 6. Or do 2 to 3 cyclic sighs.
O – Observe. Name the feeling ("This is fear," "This is frustration"). Remind yourself: "I have gotten 
through hard days before."
P – Proceed with one small step. Choose one thing from your list: a warm shower or heat pack, a short 
walk, a prayer, music, calling your support person, or resting with a guided imagery recording.
During a flare: follow the flare plan from your doctor, use heat or cold as advised, pace your activities, 
and lower your goals for the day without guilt. Call your doctor's office if the flare is severe, if you have a 
fever, or if symptoms are new or different.

Step 5: Know when to get professional help
Asking for help is a sign of strength. Talk with your doctor, or ask for a referral to a psychologist, 
counselor, social worker or psychiatrist, if:
 Stress, worry or sadness lasts more than two weeks or keeps getting worse.
 You have lost interest or pleasure in things you used to enjoy.
 Stress or low mood is affecting your sleep, eating, work, relationships or ability to take your 
medicines.
 You are using alcohol, drugs or extra pain medicine to cope.
 You have panic attacks, or memories of a past trauma that will not go away.
 Your pain feels out of control or is spreading.

Quiet the Mind Every Day
Of all the steps in this paper, the most important is the simplest: give your mind a few minutes of quiet 
every day. Daily practice is what trains the stress system to settle more quickly. It is like brushing your 
teeth: a little every day does far more than a lot once in a while.
You can quiet the mind through meditation, prayer, slow breathing, a body scan, a quiet walk, gentle Tai 
Chi, reading a meaningful passage, or simply sitting with a cup of tea and watching the sky. Choose what 
feels right for you. The three short scripts below can be read aloud by a family member, recorded on 
your phone, or followed silently.
A quiet moment. Photo: Rolf Schweizer, CC BY 2.0, via 
Wikimedia Commons.
Tips for a daily practice
 Same time, same place: link it to something you already do, 
such as morning coffee or bedtime.
 Sit or lie comfortably. Use pillows to support sore joints.
 Turn off your phone's sound, or use it only to play a 
recording.
 Wandering thoughts are normal. Each time you notice and 
return, you are strengthening the mind.
 Five minutes counts. Two minutes counts. Showing up is 
what matters.
“Breathing in, I calm my body. Breathing out, I smile. Dwelling in the present 
moment, I know this is a wonderful moment.”
— Thich Nhat Hanh, Being Peace (1987)
Script 1: A 5-minute calming breath
1. Sit comfortably with your feet flat on the floor, or lie down. Let your hands rest in your lap or on your 
belly. Close your eyes or lower your gaze.
2. Take one deep breath in through your nose and let it out slowly through your mouth, like a sigh.
3. Now breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 4. Feel your belly rise under your hand.
4. Breathe out slowly for a count of 6, as if you were blowing through a straw. Feel your belly fall.
5. Keep going: in for 4, out for 6. If it helps, say silently "calm" as you breathe in and "release" as you 
breathe out.
6. When your mind wanders, notice where it went, and kindly bring your attention back to the breath.
7. After about 5 minutes (about 30 breaths), let your breathing return to normal. Notice how your body 
feels. Open your eyes slowly.
If you feel dizzy or short of breath, stop and breathe normally. Slow breathing should feel easy, never 
forced.

Script 2: A 10-minute body scan
1. Lie on your back or sit in a supportive chair. Rest your arms by your sides. Take three slow breaths.
2. Bring your attention to your toes. Notice what is there: warmth, coolness, tingling, ache or nothing at 
all. Just notice, without trying to change it.
3. Slowly move your attention up: the soles of your feet, ankles, calves, knees and thighs. Spend about 30 
seconds on each area.
4. If you come to an area of pain or stiffness, breathe gently toward it. Imagine the breath softening the 
space around it. You do not need to fight it.
5. Continue to your hips, lower back, belly, chest and upper back. Then your hands, wrists, elbows, 
shoulders and neck.
6. Notice your jaw, cheeks, eyes and forehead. Let them soften.
7. Finally, feel your whole body breathing, from head to toe, as one. Rest here for a few breaths.
8. Wiggle your fingers and toes, take a deep breath and slowly return to your day, or drift into sleep.
Script 3: A short prayer or reflection (for any faith or none)
1. Sit quietly and take three slow breaths. Place a hand on your heart if that feels comforting.
2. Give thanks: Bring to mind one thing you are grateful for today, however small.
3. Let go: Name one worry you are carrying. Silently say, "I release what I cannot control," or offer it in 
prayer in your own words.
4. Ask for strength: "May I have patience with my body. May I have courage for what I can change. May I 
be at peace."
5. Think of others: Send a kind wish or a prayer to someone you love, or to someone who is struggling.
6. Rest in stillness: Sit in silence for one minute. If you pray, you may end with a prayer from your own 
tradition.

videos, social media posts and in-app features, is provided for general educational purposes only. It is not 
a substitute for individualized medical advice, diagnosis or treatment.
The practices described here are meant to support, not replace, the medical care and medicines 
prescribed by your doctor. Do not stop or change any medicine without talking with your doctor. Talk to 
your doctor or a licensed mental health professional (such as a psychologist, counselor, social worker or 
psychiatrist) if stress, worry, sadness or trouble coping lasts more than two weeks or affects your daily 
life.
Check with your doctor or physical therapist before starting a new exercise program, Tai Chi, qigong or 
yoga, and adapt movements to protect painful or inflamed joints. Stop any breathing exercise that makes 
you dizzy or short of breath. Expressive writing about painful experiences can bring up strong feelings; 
stop and seek support if it becomes overwhelming. People with a history of trauma may wish to learn 
meditation with a trained teacher or therapist.
