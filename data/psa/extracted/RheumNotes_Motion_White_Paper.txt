A Note From the Author
In my years of practicing rheumatology, I have learned that two of the 
most powerful treatments I can offer do not come in a pill bottle or a 
syringe. They are knowledge and movement. Medicines have 
transformed what is possible for people with rheumatoid arthritis, 
psoriatic arthritis, spondyloarthritis and the many other conditions 
we treat. But the people who do best over the long run are almost 
always the ones who keep moving, in whatever way their bodies 
allow.
I also know how hard that can be. When your knees ache, your hands 
are stiff in the morning, or fatigue sits on you like a heavy coat, "just 
exercise more" is not helpful advice. It can feel like one more thing 
you are failing at. That is exactly why the theme of this component is 
progress, not perfection. Five minutes in a chair counts. One more 
stand-up from the couch than last week counts. Starting again after a 
flare counts most of all.
Rheumy, the RheumNotes© chatbot 
guide, will help patients find the right 
routine, set goals, and celebrate small 
wins.
The RheumNotes© Motion Component is designed to meet people where they are. It brings together 
plain-language articles, friendly illustrations, short videos led by the RheumNotes© physical therapist, 
social media content and visual mind maps, organized by topic and by body region. It is written for 
patients, but also for spouses, adult children, friends and caregivers, because movement is easier when 
someone is doing it with you.
This content is meant to be used by any rheumatology or medical practice. It is general by design, and it 
is never a substitute for the individual advice of your own clinician or therapist. Please talk with your 
care team before starting a new program, especially if you have had a recent flare, surgery, a fall, or a 
change in your health.
Wherever you are starting from, I hope this helps you take the next small step.
Frederick T. Murphy, DO, FACP, FACR

Executive Summary
Movement is one of the most consistently recommended non-drug treatments in rheumatology. The 
2019 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/Arthritis Foundation guideline for osteoarthritis of the 
hand, hip and knee strongly recommends exercise, tai chi (for hip and knee OA) and self-efficacy and 
self-management programs. The 2022 ACR guideline on exercise, rehabilitation, diet and integrative 
interventions for rheumatoid arthritis strongly recommends consistent engagement in exercise, 
alongside disease-modifying medicines. Yet many patients do not know where to start, are afraid of 
making things worse, or have been given advice that does not fit their bodies or their lives.
The RheumNotes© Motion Component closes that gap. It extends the RheumNotes© platform, which 
already delivers plain-language, multi-format, clinician-reviewed education, into the everyday work of 
moving with arthritis. It is built around six pillars:
 Foundations: why movement matters, how physical and occupational therapy help, and how to 
protect joints.
 Exercise types: isotonic resistance training with bands and light weights, Tai Chi, gentle and adaptive 
yoga, and chair-based exercise.
 Safety: flares, hot swollen joints, osteoporosis, joint replacement, and clear "stop and call" red flags.
 Self-management: pacing, fatigue, and behavior change built on the principle of progress, not 
perfection.
 Nine regional modules: neck, shoulders, elbows, spine, hips, knees, ankles, feet, and hands and 
wrists, each with common conditions, plain-language modalities, starter exercises (always including 
a chair option), an illustration and a mind map.
 A multi-format content plan: article titles, illustration lists, YouTube series and scripts, social posts 
for Instagram, Facebook, X and TikTok, and mind maps for every region and topic.
Content is written at about a 6th–8th grade reading level, uses diverse, age-appropriate imagery of 
middle-aged and older adults, and features the RheumNotes© physical therapist as the trusted on-
screen guide. Every piece of content is intended for review by clinicians before publication and is general 
enough for any practice to adopt.
Important
This white paper and the content it describes are for education only. They are not a substitute for 
individualized medical advice, diagnosis or treatment. Patients should check with their own clinician or 
physical therapist before starting any new exercise program.

Why Movement Matters in Arthritis
For many years, people with arthritis were told to rest their joints. We now know that, for most people, 
the opposite is true. Regular, appropriate movement is part of treatment for osteoarthritis, rheumatoid 
arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis and many other rheumatic diseases.
What movement does for your joints and body
Benefit In plain language
Joint nourishment Cartilage has no blood supply of its own. Gentle loading and unloading helps move 
joint fluid in and out, which is why people say "motion is lotion."
Strength Strong muscles act like shock absorbers and take stress off the joints.
Flexibility Moving joints through their range helps prevent stiffness and keeps everyday tasks 
possible.
Balance Balance work lowers the risk of falls, which matters more as we age or if bones are 
thin.
Heart and whole-body 
health
Many forms of inflammatory arthritis raise heart risk. Activity supports heart health, 
weight, blood sugar and blood pressure.
Mood, sleep and fatigue Regular activity often improves mood, sleep and energy, even though it may feel like 
the last thing you want to do.
The four building blocks
General physical activity guidance applies to most people with arthritis, adjusted to their abilities: 
aerobic activity (walking, cycling, water exercise), strengthening, flexibility, and balance. The U.S. 
Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans advise adults to aim for about 150 minutes per week of 
moderate activity plus muscle strengthening on at least two days, and note that people with chronic 
conditions should be as active as their abilities and conditions allow. Some activity is better than none.
Progress, not perfection
You do not have to hit any target on day one. If 150 minutes a week sounds impossible, start with 5 
minutes a day. That is progress, and it counts.

Modalities in Plain Language
"Modalities" are tools and treatments used alongside exercise to ease pain and make movement easier. 
They are helpers, not replacements for active exercise.
Modality What it is What patients should know
Heat Warm packs, warm shower, paraffin 
wax for hands
Best before activity to loosen stiff joints. 15–20 
minutes. Avoid on hot, swollen joints or numb skin.
Cold Ice pack or frozen vegetables 
wrapped in a towel
Best after activity or for a sore, swollen area. 10–15 
minutes. Protect the skin.
TENS Small device sending a mild tingling 
current through skin pads
Some people find short-term comfort. The 2019 
ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline recommends 
against TENS for knee and hip OA, so ask your 
clinician or PT.
Therapeutic 
ultrasound
Sound waves applied with a wand 
and gel by a therapist
A passive treatment with limited evidence. Active 
exercise is preferred.
Splints and orthotics Supports for hands, wrists, feet and 
ankles
Hand orthoses for thumb-base OA are strongly 
recommended in the 2019 OA guideline. Foot 
orthotics can spread pressure.
Bracing Knee, ankle or back supports Knee braces for tibiofemoral OA are strongly 
recommended in the 2019 OA guideline; fitting 
matters.
Taping Elastic or rigid tape applied to the 
skin
May provide support or feedback; conditionally 
recommended for thumb-base OA.
Activity modification Changing how, when or how much 
you do something
Not "stop doing it" but "do it smarter": shorter 
bouts, better tools, better positions.

Isotonic Resistance Exercise
Seated band row: a joint-friendly way to strengthen the upper back.
Isotonic exercise means the muscle moves the joint through a range of motion against resistance, like 
lifting a light weight or stretching a band. Strengthening is one of the best ways to protect joints and 
stay independent. The 2022 ACR RA guideline conditionally recommends consistent resistance exercise 
and emphasizes that it should be prescribed and supervised appropriately by physical therapists or other 
qualified professionals.
Equipment
 Resistance bands and tubes: inexpensive, portable and color-coded from light to heavy (colors vary 
by brand). Loop handles help sore hands.
 Light hand weights: 1–5 lb dumbbells, or household items such as soup cans or water bottles.
 Ankle weights: light cuff weights for seated knee and hip exercises.
 Body weight: sit-to-stands, wall push-ups and heel raises.
Sets, reps and rest
Element Starting point (adjust with your PT)
Repetitions 8–12 slow, controlled reps; start with 5 if that is what feels right
Sets 1 set to start; build to 2–3
Speed About 2 seconds up, 3 seconds down; no jerking

Element Starting point (adjust with your PT)
Effort "Moderate": you could do a few more reps at the end of the set
Frequency 2–3 non-consecutive days per week for each muscle group
Rest About a minute between sets; a rest day between strength sessions
The progression ladder
1.  Master the movement with no resistance.
2.  Add reps, up to 12–15.
3.  Add a second, then third set.
4.  Move to the next band color or add 1 lb.
5.  Drop back down a step whenever you need to; that is still progress.
Pain rules
The 2-hour pain rule
A widely used rule of thumb among therapists: if your joint pain is noticeably worse 2 hours after 
exercise than it was before, you did a little too much. Next time, reduce the reps, resistance or range, but 
do not stop altogether.
 During exercise: mild discomfort or muscle effort is OK. Sharp, stabbing or rapidly increasing pain is 
a signal to stop that movement.
 The next-day check: more stiffness or swelling the next morning than usual suggests the dose was 
too high.
 Muscle soreness versus joint pain: a dull ache in the muscle a day or two after a new exercise is 
normal; joint swelling is not.
 During a flare: switch to gentle range-of-motion exercise for the affected joints.

Exercise Muscle group Start
Sit-to-stand Thighs, hips 1 × 5–10
Seated band row Upper back 1 × 10
Wall push-up Chest, arms 1 × 8
Seated knee extension Thighs 1 × 10 each
Standing heel raise at counter Calves, balance 1 × 10
Band outward rotation Shoulders 1 × 10

Tai Chi
Tai Chi combines slow, flowing movement, weight shifting and calm breathing.
Tai Chi is a traditional Chinese mind-body practice of slow, flowing movements, gentle weight shifts and 
focused breathing. It builds strength, balance and body awareness, and many people find it calming. The 
2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline strongly recommends Tai Chi for people with knee and hip 
osteoarthritis, and the 2022 ACR RA guideline conditionally recommends mind-body exercise, including 
Tai Chi and qigong.
Why it suits arthritis
 Low impact and adaptable; can be done standing, with a chair for support, or fully seated.
 Trains balance through slow weight shifting, which may help with fall prevention.
 Combines movement with relaxation and breathing, which can help with stress and pain coping.
 Social: classes are often available at community centers and senior centers, and online.
Getting started
 Look for beginner or arthritis-adapted classes and instructors experienced with older adults.
 Start with 10–15 minutes and build up.
 Keep knees soft and slightly bent, but avoid deep knee bends that cause pain.
 Seated Tai Chi is a full option, not a lesser one.

Yoga: Gentle, Chair and Adaptive
Adaptive yoga uses props such as blocks, straps and blankets so each pose fits the body.
Yoga combines postures, breathing and relaxation. For people with arthritis, the best yoga is gentle and 
adapted to the body. The 2019 OA guideline conditionally recommends yoga for knee OA, and the 2022 
RA guideline includes yoga among recommended mind-body exercises.
Types of yoga that fit
Style What it looks like
Gentle yoga Slow pace, simple poses, lots of rest and breathing.
Chair yoga All poses done seated or standing with a chair for support. Excellent for balance 
concerns or limited floor mobility.
Adaptive yoga Uses props (blocks, straps, bolsters, blankets) and modifications so poses fit each 
person's joints and abilities.
Restorative yoga Supported, resting poses held longer for relaxation.
Safety tips
 Tell the instructor about your arthritis, joint replacements and osteoporosis.
 Skip or modify poses that put heavy weight on painful wrists, knees or the neck (for example, 
headstands and deep kneeling).
 With osteoporosis, avoid deep forward folds and forceful twists.
 "Comfortable effort" is the goal; you should be able to breathe smoothly.

Chair and Seated Exercise
Chair exercise is real exercise, and it can be done almost anywhere.
Chair-based exercise opens the door for people with balance problems, severe joint pain, fatigue, recent 
flares, or limited mobility, and for anyone who wants a safe place to start. A sturdy, armless chair 
without wheels, placed on a non-slip surface, is all that is needed.

Safety and Red Flags
Exercise is very safe for most people with arthritis when it is matched to their condition. These 
guardrails help patients and caregivers know when to adjust, and when to stop and call.
Traffic-light guide
What you notice What to do
GREEN: keep going Mild muscle effort; slight discomfort that 
settles within 2 hours
Continue; progress slowly
YELLOW: adjust Pain worse 2+ hours later; more morning 
stiffness; mild new swelling
Reduce reps, resistance or range; add 
rest; switch to gentle motion
RED: stop and call See the red-flag list below Stop exercising and contact your 
clinician, or call 911 for an emergency
Flares and hot, swollen joints
During a flare, inflamed joints are more vulnerable. Keep them moving gently through a comfortable 
range a few times a day, but avoid resistance and high-impact activity on those joints until the flare 
settles. A single joint that becomes suddenly hot, red and very swollen, especially with fever, may be 
infected or due to gout and needs same-day medical attention.
Osteoporosis precautions
 Many people with rheumatic disease have thinner bones, sometimes due to steroid use.
 Strength, balance and posture exercises are encouraged and help reduce fall risk.
 Avoid loaded forward bending of the spine (sit-ups, crunches, toe touches), forceful twisting, and 
high-impact moves if fracture risk is high.
 Choose spine-friendly options: chest lifts, bridges, wall push-ups and balance work.
 Sudden new back pain should be checked for a compression fracture.
After joint replacement
 Follow your surgeon's and therapist's instructions exactly; precautions differ by joint and surgical 
approach.
 After some hip replacements, certain positions (such as deep bending or crossing legs) are restricted 
for a period.
 After knee replacement, regaining full straightening and bending is a key goal; work closely with 
your PT.
 Report new warmth, redness, drainage, fever or calf swelling promptly.

When to stop and call
Red flags: stop exercising and contact your clinician
• Chest pain or pressure, severe shortness of breath, fainting or a racing, irregular heartbeat (call 911).
• A joint that becomes hot, red and very swollen, especially with fever or chills.
• Sudden severe pain, a "pop," or inability to bear weight or use a limb.
• New numbness, tingling or weakness in an arm or leg; new loss of bowel or bladder control 
(emergency).
• Calf pain, swelling and warmth (possible blood clot).
• New headache, jaw pain with chewing, or vision changes, especially with polymyalgia rheumatica.
• A fall with injury, or dizziness that does not settle.

Pacing and Fatigue
Fatigue is one of the most common and frustrating symptoms of inflammatory arthritis. It is real, and it 
is not laziness. Pacing helps people get more done over the week with less crash-and-recover.
The boom-and-bust cycle
On a good day, it is tempting to do everything: clean the whole house, garden all afternoon, take a long 
walk. Then comes a day or two of pain and exhaustion. Over time, this cycle can lead to doing less and 
less. Pacing breaks the cycle by keeping activity steadier from day to day.
The 4 Ps
P What it means Example
Plan Look at the week ahead and spread out demanding 
tasks
Laundry Monday, groceries Wednesday
Prioritize Decide what must be done, what can wait, and what 
someone else can do
Ask family to carry heavy bags
Pace Break tasks into chunks with rest in between Garden 20 minutes, rest 10
Position Use body-friendly positions and tools Sit to chop vegetables; use a long-handled 
duster
Movement and fatigue
 Regular, moderate activity often improves energy over time.
 Short bouts (5–10 minutes) spread across the day count.
 Plan exercise for your best time of day; many people with RA do better later in the morning once 
stiffness eases.
 Sleep, mood, anemia, thyroid problems and disease activity can all affect fatigue; tell your clinician if 
it is getting worse.

Behavior Change and Adherence: Progress, Not 
Perfection
Knowing that exercise helps is not the same as doing it. Most people start strong and then life, pain or a 
flare gets in the way. The Motion Component treats restarting as a skill, and celebrates consistency over 
intensity. EULAR's physical activity recommendations encourage using behavior-change techniques such 
as self-monitoring, goal setting, action planning, feedback and problem solving.
Practical strategies
Strategy How it works in RheumNotes©
Start tiny Goals so small they feel almost too easy: "2 minutes of seated marching after breakfast."
Action plans What, when, where, how much. Rheumy helps patients write one each week and rate 
their confidence (aim for 7 out of 10 or higher).
Habit stacking Attach movement to something already done daily: heel raises while brushing teeth, 
quad sets while the kettle boils.
Self-monitoring Simple check-ins in the app: minutes moved, pain, energy. Trends, not grades.
Celebrate small wins Badges and messages for consistency and restarts, never just for big numbers.
Plan for setbacks A "flare-day plan" and a "restart plan" written in advance.
Social support Invite a partner, caregiver or friend; join a class or online group.
What "progress, not perfection" sounds like
"I only did half my routine today" becomes "I moved today."
"I missed a whole week" becomes "I'm starting again today."
"I can't do what I used to" becomes "I can do more than I could last month."

Regional Module 1: Neck (Cervical Spine)
The neck carries the weight of the head all day. 
Stiffness and aching are common as discs and 
small joints wear with age, and some forms of 
inflammatory arthritis can involve the neck. 
The goal is easy, relaxed motion and good 
posture, never forcing the head to its end 
range.
Progress, not perfection: Stiff neck? Move it 
gently, often, and pain-free. Small range today, 
bigger range next month.
Gentle seated neck side-bend: slow, small, and pain-free.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Cervical spondylosis (neck 
osteoarthritis)
Wear-and-tear changes in the discs and small joints. Causes stiffness and 
aching that is often worse in the morning or after long screen or driving time.
Muscle strain and myofascial neck 
pain
Tight, tender muscles across the neck and upper shoulders, often linked to 
posture, stress and poor sleep position.
Pinched nerve (cervical 
radiculopathy)

Regional Module 2: Shoulders
The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the 
body, which also makes it easy to irritate. Most 
shoulder problems respond to a mix of gentle 
range of motion, gradual strengthening of the 
rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles, and 
smart changes to daily tasks.
Progress, not perfection: Shoulder rehab rule: 
stretch, don't stab. Gentle and daily beats hard 
and rare.
Pendulum exercise: let the arm hang and swing in small circles.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Rotator cuff tendinopathy Irritation of the tendons that steady the shoulder. Pain on the outside of the 
upper arm, especially reaching overhead or sleeping on that side.
Subacromial bursitis Inflamed fluid sac above the rotator cuff, often occurring with tendinopathy.
Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) The joint lining thickens and tightens, causing pain and then marked stiffness. 
More common in people with diabetes or thyroid disease. Usually improves 
over many months with patient, gentle stretching.
Shoulder osteoarthritis Wear of the ball-and-socket joint causing deep ache, grinding and loss of 
motion.
RA and PsA involvement Inflammation of the shoulder joint lining causing warmth, swelling and 
morning stiffness.
Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) Both shoulders stiff and achy, especially in the morning, in people over 50. 
See your doctor.
Modalities, explained simply
Modality What it does
Heat before, cold after Warmth loosens the shoulder before stretching; a cold pack can settle soreness 
afterward.
Activity modification Keep frequently used items between waist and shoulder height. Use a step stool 
instead of reaching overhead.

Regional Module 3: Elbows
Elbow pain is often caused by overloaded 
tendons at the bony points on either side of 
the elbow, rather than by the joint itself. 
Inflammatory arthritis can also cause elbow 
swelling and loss of straightening. Gradual 
loading of the forearm muscles is the core of 
recovery.
Progress, not perfection: Tendons like slow, 
steady load. Rest alone rarely fixes tennis elbow.
Wrist lifts with a light weight strengthen the forearm muscles 
that attach at the elbow.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) Pain on the outer elbow with gripping, lifting or turning a doorknob. Very 
common, and most people do not play tennis.
Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow) Pain on the inner elbow with gripping or wrist bending.
Olecranon bursitis A soft swelling at the tip of the elbow. If it is red, hot or painful, it may be 
infected or due to gout, so call your clinician.
RA and PsA of the elbow Joint swelling and difficulty fully straightening the arm. Early motion helps 
preserve range.
Elbow osteoarthritis Less common; causes stiffness and catching, often in people who did heavy 
manual work.
Modalities, explained simply
Modality What it does
Counterforce strap A band worn just below the elbow that can ease tendon strain during activity.
Wrist splint Resting the wrist can unload the forearm tendons during flares or at night.
Cold 10–15 minutes after aggravating activity.

Regional Module 4: Spine (Thoracic and Lumbar)
Back pain is one of the most common reasons 
people limit activity. Most back pain is not 
dangerous, and staying active is usually better 
than bed rest. Inflammatory back pain from 
axial spondyloarthritis behaves differently from 
mechanical back pain, and both benefit from 
regular movement.
Progress, not perfection: Osteoporosis tip: 
strengthen the back by lifting the chest, not by 
doing crunches.
 Cat-cow: gently round and arch the back in rhythm with the 
breath.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Lumbar spondylosis (back 
osteoarthritis)
Age-related disc and joint changes. Stiffness and aching, often worse after 
sitting or standing still.
Spinal stenosis Narrowing around the nerves causing leg pain or heaviness with walking that 
eases when sitting or leaning forward (like on a shopping cart).
Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) Inflammatory back pain that starts before age 45, is worse with rest and in 
the morning, and improves with exercise. Posture, extension and breathing 
exercises are key.
Osteoporotic compression fracture Sudden mid-back pain, sometimes after a minor strain, in people with low 
bone density. Needs medical review and specific exercise precautions.
Muscle strain and nonspecific low 
back pain
Very common, usually improves within weeks with gentle activity.
DISH (diffuse idiopathic skeletal 
hyperostosis)
Extra bone along the spine causing stiffness, more common in older adults 
and people with diabetes.
Modalities, explained simply
Modality What it does
Heat Warmth before activity helps stiff backs, especially in the morning.
Cold Can help after a flare-up of mechanical pain.
TENS Some people use it for short-term comfort. Not a replacement for exercise.
Bracing Short-term only and as advised, for example after a compression fracture.
Activity modification Change positions often, bend at the hips and knees, keep loads close to the body.

Regional Module 5: Hips
Strong hip muscles protect the hip joint, 
support balance and make walking, stairs and 
getting up from a chair easier. Pain on the 
outside of the hip is often a tendon problem 
rather than arthritis, while groin pain more 
often comes from the joint itself.
Progress, not perfection: Cane goes in the hand 
opposite the sore hip. Small detail, big difference.
Standing side leg lift at a sturdy chair builds hip strength and 
balance.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Hip osteoarthritis Deep groin or front-of-thigh pain and stiffness, trouble putting on shoes and 
socks.
Greater trochanteric pain syndrome 
(GTPS)
Pain on the outside of the hip from irritated gluteal tendons and bursa. 
Worse lying on that side, climbing stairs or crossing legs.
Axial SpA and sacroiliac joint 
involvement
Buttock pain that may alternate sides, worse with rest.
RA and PsA of the hip Less common, but can cause groin pain and stiffness.
Osteonecrosis (avascular necrosis) Loss of blood supply to the hip bone, a rare risk with long-term steroid use.

seat or cushion if low chairs are hard.
Weight management For hip OA with excess weight, even modest weight loss can lighten the load on

Regional Module 6: Knees
Knee osteoarthritis is one of the best-studied 
areas for exercise. Strong thigh and hip 
muscles act like shock absorbers. Many forms 
of activity help, so the best exercise is the one 
you will actually do.
Progress, not perfection: For knee OA, the best 
exercise is the one you'll keep doing.
Seated knee extension strengthens the thigh muscles that 
protect the knee.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Knee osteoarthritis Pain with walking, stairs and getting up, plus morning stiffness that usually 
lasts less than 30 minutes.
Patellofemoral pain Pain around or behind the kneecap with stairs, squatting or sitting with knees 
bent for a long time.
Baker's cyst A fluid pocket behind the knee, often linked to arthritis. Sudden calf swelling 
needs urgent review to rule out a blood clot.
Pes anserine bursitis Tenderness on the inner knee just below the joint line.
RA, PsA and gout Warm, swollen knee with fluid. Exercise is adjusted during flares.

Modality What it does
Knee brace Unloader-style braces for inner or outer knee OA, and kneecap braces for 
patellofemoral pain, can help some people. Ask your PT to fit one.
Taping Kneecap taping may ease pain short-term during activity.
Cane Held in the opposite hand, a cane can reduce knee load.
Heat and cold Heat for stiffness; cold for a swollen, sore knee after activity.
Footwear Cushioned, supportive shoes. Avoid high heels.
Weight management For knee OA with excess weight, losing even a modest amount can reduce pain.

Starter exercises

Regional Module 7: Ankles
Healthy ankles give balance and a confident 
step. Ankle and heel problems can come from 
old sprains, tendon overload, or inflammatory 
arthritis that targets where tendons attach to 
bone.
Progress, not perfection: Achilles tendons love 
slow heel lowering. Count to three on the way 
down.
Heel raises at the counter: hands on the counter for balance, 
rise slowly, lower slowly.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Achilles tendinopathy Pain and stiffness in the cord at the back of the heel, worse first thing in the 
morning or when starting activity.
Achilles enthesitis in SpA and PsA Inflammation where the tendon attaches to the heel bone, a clue to 
spondyloarthritis.
Ankle osteoarthritis Often follows an old injury; causes stiffness and swelling.
Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction Inner-ankle pain and a slowly flattening arch.
RA ankle and hindfoot involvement Swelling and pain that make walking on uneven ground hard.
Chronic ankle instability Frequent "rolling" of the ankle after past sprains.

Modality What it does
Supportive shoes and heel lifts A small heel lift can unload a sore Achilles.
Ankle brace Lace-up or stirrup braces for instability or arthritis.
Orthotics Arch supports for flattening arches.
Cold 10–15 minutes after activity for a sore tendon.
Taping Some PTs tape for extra support during activity.

Regional Module 8: Feet
Feet carry us everywhere, and foot pain 
changes how we walk, which can strain the 
knees, hips and back. Good footwear, orthotics 
and simple daily stretches go a long way.
Progress, not perfection: Swollen "sausage" toe? 
That's worth a call to your rheumatology team.
Rolling the arch over a small ball is a gentle, seated self-care 
option for plantar fasciitis.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Plantar fasciitis Sharp heel pain with the first steps in the morning or after sitting.
RA forefoot involvement Pain in the balls of the feet, sometimes described as walking on pebbles.
Hallux rigidus (big-toe arthritis) Stiff, painful big toe, especially when pushing off.
Bunions and hammertoes Changes in toe alignment that make shoes uncomfortable.
Gout Sudden, severe, red, hot swelling, often at the big toe. Needs medical 
treatment.
PsA dactylitis ("sausage toe") A whole toe swells. Tell your rheumatology team.
Modalities, explained simply
Modality What it does
Orthotics Over-the-counter or custom inserts support the arch and spread pressure.
Shoes Wide toe box, cushioned sole, firm heel counter. Rocker-bottom soles can help a 
stiff big toe.
Metatarsal pads Small pads that take pressure off the balls of the feet.
Night splint Holds the foot gently flexed overnight for stubborn plantar fasciitis.
Cold Roll the arch over a frozen water bottle for 5–10 minutes.

Regional Module 9: Hands and Wrists
Hands are how we care for ourselves and 
others. Hand arthritis affects buttons, jars, keys 
and phones. The aim is to keep the fingers 
moving, protect the joints during daily tasks, 
and use smart tools so the hands last.
Progress, not perfection: Push doors with your 
palm, not your fingers. Joint protection is all 
about small swaps.
Gentle putty squeezes support grip, but go easy during a flare.
Common conditions
Condition What it means
Hand osteoarthritis Bony bumps at the end and middle finger joints and pain at the base of the 
thumb (CMC joint), especially with pinching.
De Quervain's tenosynovitis Pain on the thumb side of the wrist with lifting or twisting, common in new 
parents and grandparents.
Trigger finger A finger catches or locks when bending, then snaps straight.
Carpal tunnel syndrome Numbness and tingling in the thumb, index and middle fingers, often at night.
Rheumatoid arthritis Swelling of the knuckles and wrists with morning stiffness lasting more than 
30 minutes.
Psoriatic arthritis Can affect the end finger joints, cause whole-finger swelling (dactylitis) and 
nail changes.
Modalities, explained simply
Modality What it does
Thumb-base (CMC) splint A hand orthosis for thumb-base OA can ease pain and improve function.
Wrist splint Worn at night for carpal tunnel or for wrist rest during RA flares.
Warm water or paraffin Warmth before exercise loosens stiff fingers.
Kinesiology tape Sometimes used for thumb-base OA; your hand therapist can show you.
Joint protection Use the largest, strongest joints for the job: carry bags on the forearm, push doors 
with the palm.
Adaptive tools Jar openers, built-up utensils, key turners, button hooks and lever door handles.

Starter exercises
Do these slowly, within a comfortable range, and stop any move that causes sharp pain. Start with the lower 
number of repetitions.
1.  Tendon glides (chair-based at a table). Move through five positions: straight hand, hook fist, flat fist, 
full fist, straight fingers. Hold each 3 seconds. 5 rounds.
2.  Make an O. Touch the thumb to each fingertip in turn, making a round O. 5 rounds.
3.  Wrist bends on a table. Forearm on the table, hand over the edge. Bend the wrist up and down 
gently. 10 times.
4.  Finger walks. Hand flat on the table, walk each finger toward the thumb side. 5 rounds.
5.  Gentle putty squeeze. Squeeze soft putty or a sponge for 3 seconds, then relax. 10 times. Skip during 
flares.
Safety note
During a flare, keep to gentle motion without resistance. Wrist splints should not cause numbness. New 
hand weakness or constant numbness needs evaluation.

Evidence Base
The Motion Component is grounded in major rheumatology guidelines. Only well-established, verified 
guidance is cited below; no statistics have been added beyond what these sources state.
Guideline Key points relevant to the Motion Component
2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation 
Guideline for OA of the Hand, Hip 
and Knee
Strongly recommends exercise for knee, hip and hand OA; supervised programs and 
pairing with self-management tend to be more effective.
Strongly recommends tai chi (knee/hip OA), self-efficacy and self-management 
programs, cane use, hand orthoses for thumb-base (first CMC) OA, and tibiofemoral 
knee bracing.
Conditionally recommends balance exercise, yoga, kinesiotaping for first CMC OA, 
patellofemoral bracing and thermal interventions; recommends against TENS for 
knee/hip OA.
2022 ACR Guideline for Exercise, 
Rehabilitation, Diet and Additional 
Integrative Interventions for RA
Strongly recommends consistent engagement in exercise, used together with 
DMARDs.
Conditionally recommends aerobic, aquatic, resistance and mind-body exercise (yoga, 
Tai Chi, qigong), and comprehensive PT and OT.
Conditionally recommends rehabilitation interventions such as hand exercises, 
splints/orthoses, and joint protection; stresses shared decision-making.
2018 EULAR Recommendations for 
Physical Activity in Inflammatory 
Arthritis and OA
Physical activity should be an integral part of standard care for RA, SpA, and hip/knee 
OA.
General activity recommendations (aerobic, strength, flexibility, neuromotor) are 
applicable; plans should be shared decisions.
Supports behavior-change techniques such as goal setting, self-monitoring and action 
planning.
2019 ACR/SAA/SPARTAN 
Recommendations for Axial 
Spondyloarthritis
Strongly recommends physical therapy over no physical therapy.
Conditionally recommends active PT (supervised exercise) over passive interventions 
(massage, ultrasound, heat).
Physical Activity Guidelines for 
Americans, 2nd edition (2018)
Adults: about 150 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity and muscle 
strengthening on 2 or more days; older adults add balance training.
People with chronic conditions should be as active as their abilities and conditions 
allow.
