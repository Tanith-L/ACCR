A Note From the Author
"I'm so tired." I hear these words from patients with arthritis and 
rheumatic disease almost every day. Pain keeps people awake. Poor 
sleep makes pain feel worse the next day. Over time, the two feed 
each other, and fatigue can become one of the hardest parts of living 
with a chronic illness.
That is why the theme of this component is rest is medicine. Sleep is 
not lost time. While you sleep, your body repairs tissue, balances 
hormones, sorts memories and resets the immune system. Good 
sleep will not replace the medicines your doctor prescribes, but it 
helps your body heal and helps you cope with pain.
Rheumy, the RheumNotes© chatbot 
guide, can help patients keep a sleep 
diary and build a bedtime routine.
This white paper explains sleep in plain language. It covers how much sleep people need at each age, 
what makes sleep restful, the stages of sleep, how sleep supports the immune system, and simple habits 
that help most people sleep better. It also includes tips made for people with joint pain and stiffness, 
and clear signs that it is time to talk with a doctor about a sleep problem.
This content is written for patients, families and caregivers, and it is meant to be used by any 
rheumatology or medical practice. It is general by design and is never a substitute for advice from your 
own doctor, especially before you change when or how you take any medicine.
Small changes add up. A steady bedtime, a darker bedroom or one less late cup of coffee can be the first 
step. I hope this helps you rest and restore.
Frederick T. Murphy, DO, FACP, FACR
Rheumatologist, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania

Key Takeaways
Ten things to remember
1. Sleep is a pillar of health, like food and movement. The American Heart Association now counts sleep 
as one of the eight key measures of heart health.
2. Most adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night. For ages 18 to 64 the usual range is 7 to 9 hours; 
for 65 and older, 7 to 8 hours.
3. Restorative sleep is more than hours. It also means sleep that is unbroken, comes at a regular time, 
and leaves you feeling refreshed.
4. Sleep comes in cycles of about 90 minutes. Each cycle moves through light sleep (N1, N2), deep sleep 
(N3) and dream sleep (REM). Most adults have 4 to 6 cycles a night.
5. Pain and sleep feed each other. Poor sleep lowers the pain threshold the next day, and pain breaks up 
sleep. Improving either one can help the other.
6. Sleep supports the immune system. Short or poor sleep is linked to higher inflammation markers, 
weaker vaccine responses and more colds.
7. Keep a steady schedule. Go to bed and get up at about the same time every day, including weekends. 
Get bright light in the morning.
8. Make the bedroom cool, dark and quiet. Stop screens about 30 minutes before bed. Avoid caffeine in 
the afternoon and alcohol near bedtime.
9. For insomnia, CBT-I comes first. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is the first-line treatment 
recommended by the American College of Physicians and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine.
10. Talk to your doctor about loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing, an urge to move your legs at 
night, insomnia that lasts more than 3 months, or medicines (such as prednisone) that may be keeping 
you awake.

Rest Is Medicine: Sleep as a Pillar of Wellness
We often talk about three pillars of good health: eating well, moving every day and sleeping well. Sleep 
is the one people give up first when life gets busy. Yet the brain and body do some of their most 
important work while we sleep.
Figure 2. Sleep is an active time of repair for the brain and body. Photo: Kalegin Michail, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.
What sleep does for you
Body system What happens during good sleep
Brain and memory The brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day.
Research in animals shows the brain clears waste products faster during sleep.
Muscles, joints and tissue Growth hormone is released, mostly during deep sleep. It helps repair muscle and 
tissue.
Immune system Sleep helps the immune system remember germs and respond well to vaccines.
Steady sleep helps keep inflammation in balance.
Heart and blood vessels Heart rate and blood pressure fall during most of the night, giving the heart a rest.
Metabolism and weight Sleep helps balance hunger hormones and blood sugar. Short sleep is linked to 
weight gain and type 2 diabetes.
Mood and coping Sleep helps us handle stress, pain and emotions. Long-term insomnia about 
doubles the risk of later depression.

Sleep and heart health
In 2022 the American Heart Association added sleep to its list of the most important measures of heart 
health, now called Life's Essential 8. The other seven are diet, physical activity, nicotine, weight, 
cholesterol, blood sugar and blood pressure. For adults, 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night earns the top score. 
This matters in rheumatology because people with rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, psoriatic arthritis and 
gout already have a higher risk of heart disease.
Large studies that follow people for many years also show that both very short sleep and very long sleep 
are linked with a higher risk of early death. Short sleep was linked with about a 12% higher risk. Long 
sleep was linked with about a 30% higher risk, though very long sleep is often a sign of another illness 
rather than the cause.
Rest is medicine
Think of a good night's sleep as a daily dose of repair. One bad night will not harm you, but a steady 
pattern of good sleep, night after night, helps protect your joints, heart, brain and mood.

Sleep and Arthritis: Why It Matters Even More
Many people with arthritis and other rheumatic diseases sleep poorly. Joint pain, stiffness, medicines, 
worry and low mood can all get in the way. Studies of people with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) have found 
that poor sleep goes along with more pain, more disability and higher disease activity.
The two-way pain–sleep cycle
Pain and sleep affect each other in both directions. Pain makes it hard to fall asleep and causes you to 
wake up during the night. In turn, a poor night's sleep lowers your pain threshold (the point at which 
something starts to hurt), so the same joint hurts more the next day. Reviews of many studies suggest 
that poor sleep may predict next-day pain even more strongly than pain predicts poor sleep.
Figure 3. The pain–sleep cycle. Better sleep and better pain control can each help break it.
Researchers have shown this cycle in the lab. In the 1970s, Canadian researchers woke healthy people 
every time they entered deep sleep. After a few nights, these healthy volunteers developed muscle 
aches and tender spots much like those in fibromyalgia. In another study, people with RA who were kept 
awake for part of one night reported more fatigue, more depression and more painful joints the next 
day. When healthy adults slept only 4 hours a night for 10 nights, their levels of the inflammatory 
messenger IL-6 rose, and so did their pain.
Fatigue in rheumatic disease
Fatigue is a deep tiredness that does not go away with rest. It is one of the most common and most 
troubling symptoms of rheumatic disease. Poor sleep is only one cause. Disease activity, anemia, thyroid 
problems, low mood, some medicines and being less active can all add to it. That is why fatigue deserves 
a full conversation with your doctor.

Sleep and mood
Sleep and mood are closely linked. A review of 21 long-term studies found that people with insomnia 
had about twice the risk of developing depression later. Depression and anxiety, in turn, make sleep 
worse and make pain harder to bear. If you feel down, hopeless or worried most days, tell your doctor. 
Treating mood problems often helps both sleep and pain.
Some research also suggests that sleep problems may be linked with a higher risk of developing 
autoimmune diseases. In a large Taiwanese study, people with sleep disorders (not including sleep 
apnea) were about 1.5 times as likely to be diagnosed with an autoimmune disease, such as RA or lupus, 
over the following years. This kind of study shows a link, not proof of cause.

How Much Sleep Do You Need? Evidence-Based 
Guidelines
Experts from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), the Sleep Research Society (SRS) and 
the National Sleep Foundation (NSF) reviewed hundreds of studies to decide how much sleep people 
need. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) uses these numbers. Sleep needs change 
with age.

What "restorative sleep" means
Restorative sleep is sleep that leaves your body and mind refreshed. Hours matter, but they are only 
one part. Experts describe four parts of healthy, restorative sleep:
Part What it means A healthy target for most adults
1. Duration Enough total sleep 7 or more hours a night (7–9 hours for ages 18–64; 7–8 
hours for 65 and older)
2. Continuity Sleep that is not broken up Falling asleep within about 30 minutes; no more than 1 
waking longer than 5 minutes; 20 minutes or less awake 
after first falling asleep (up to about 30 minutes for older 
adults)
3. Timing Sleeping at the right time for your 
body clock
Mostly at night, at about the same time every day, 
including weekends
4. Quality How well you sleep and how you 
feel
Spending at least 85% of your time in bed asleep, and 
waking up feeling rested
Targets adapted from the National Sleep Foundation's sleep quality recommendations (Ohayon et al., 2017) and the AASM/SRS adult 
consensus (Watson et al., 2015). These are guides, not strict rules.
You also need enough of each stage of sleep, especially deep sleep and REM sleep. When sleep is cut 
short, the last REM periods of the morning are lost. When sleep is broken up by pain, breathing pauses 
or noise, deep sleep is lost. The next section explains these stages.

The Stages of Sleep
Sleep is not one long, even state. All night long, your brain moves through different stages. Doctors 
measure them with a sleep study, also called a polysomnogram. Small sensors on the scalp record brain 
waves (this recording is called an EEG, or electroencephalogram). Other sensors record eye movements, 
muscle activity, breathing, heart rhythm and oxygen levels.
Figure 5. A person wired for an overnight sleep study (polysomnography). Photo: Clinique Somnomed, CC BY-SA 4.0, via 
Wikimedia Commons.
Two main types of sleep
 Non-REM sleep has three stages: N1, N2 and N3. The "N" stands for non-REM. Sleep gets deeper 
from N1 to N3.
 REM sleep stands for rapid eye movement sleep. The eyes dart back and forth under closed lids. 
This is when most vivid dreams happen.

Sleep cycles: about 90 minutes each
The stages follow a pattern called a sleep cycle. A cycle usually goes N1 → N2 → N3 → N2 → REM. The 
first cycle lasts about 70 to 100 minutes. Later cycles last about 90 to 120 minutes. On average, each 
cycle is about 90 minutes long, and most adults go through 4 to 6 cycles in a night of 7 to 9 hours.
The make-up of each cycle changes as the night goes on. Deep N3 sleep happens mostly in the first third 
of the night. REM periods get longer toward morning. This is why both going to bed late and waking up 
too early can rob you of important sleep. A graph of the stages through the night is called a hypnogram.

How sleep changes with age
A large review of sleep studies from childhood to old age found that, as adults get older, total sleep time 
and sleep efficiency go down, and people spend less time in deep N3 sleep and wake up more often 
during the night. The share of REM sleep drops only a little. This is normal. But feeling unrefreshed, 
sleepy in the daytime or lying awake for long periods is not just a normal part of aging, and it is worth 
discussing with a doctor.
How long should I sleep to feel restored?
For most adults, 7 to 9 hours allows 4 to 6 full cycles, with enough deep sleep early in the night and 
enough REM sleep toward morning.
Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day helps your body clock line up the stages the right 
way.
Sleep that is broken up by pain, a full bladder, breathing pauses or noise loses deep sleep, even if the 
total hours look fine.

Sleep and the Immune System
Your immune system is your body's defense force against germs. In autoimmune diseases such as RA 
and lupus, the immune system is overactive and attacks the body's own tissues. Sleep and the immune 
system talk to each other all the time. Scientists call this the sleep–immune crosstalk.
Key words
Term Plain-language meaning
Cytokines Chemical messengers that immune cells use to talk to each other. Some, such as 
interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), turn inflammation up. Several 
modern arthritis medicines work by blocking them.
Inflammation markers Blood tests that show how much inflammation is in the body. C-reactive protein (CRP) is 
one of the most common.
Antibodies Proteins the immune system makes to recognize and fight a specific germ. Vaccines 
work by teaching the body to make them.
Immune memory The immune system's ability to remember a germ or vaccine and respond faster the next 
time.
What happens to the immune system while you sleep
 During early-night deep sleep, levels of the stress hormone cortisol are low and growth hormone 
and prolactin are high. This mix helps immune cells move to the lymph nodes, where they "learn" 
about germs and vaccines.
 Sleep appears to help turn short-term immune responses into longer-lasting immune memory, 
much as it helps the brain store memories.
 When you are fighting an infection, the immune system releases cytokines that make you sleepy. 
This is one reason rest helps recovery.
Sleep loss turns up inflammation
Many studies show that short or poor sleep pushes the immune system toward inflammation. This 
matters for people with inflammatory arthritis, whose immune systems already lean that way.

Why this matters in rheumatic disease
IL-6 and TNF are the same messengers that drive joint inflammation in RA and that some biologic 
medicines block. Sleep loss turning these messengers up may be one reason flares, pain and fatigue are 
often worse after bad nights. Treating insomnia may help calm inflammation, too.
Sleep and vaccines
Vaccines protect against flu, pneumonia, shingles, COVID-19 and other infections. This is important for 
people who take medicines that lower the immune response. Sleep helps vaccines work better.
Study What researchers found
Spiegel and colleagues, 2002 Young men limited to 4 hours of sleep for 6 nights made less than half as many flu 
antibodies 10 days after vaccination as men who slept normally.
Lange and colleagues, 2003 Adults who slept the night after a hepatitis A shot had about twice as many 
antibodies 4 weeks later as those who stayed awake that night.
Prather and colleagues, 2012 In 125 middle-aged adults getting the hepatitis B vaccine series, shorter sleep 
(measured with a wrist monitor) meant fewer antibodies and a lower chance of 
being protected 6 months later.
Spiegel and colleagues, 2023 
(review)
Across studies, short sleep (less than 6 hours a night) measured with devices was 
linked with clearly weaker antibody responses, especially in men.
Practical tip
Try to get a full night of sleep for a few nights before and after a vaccine. Ask your doctor which vaccines 
are right for you and how they fit with your arthritis medicines.
Sleep and catching colds
In two careful studies, healthy volunteers had cold virus drops placed in their noses and were watched 
to see who got sick.

autoimmune disease, sleep is part of caring for the immune system.

Sleep Hygiene: Practical Guidelines and Principles
Sleep hygiene means the daily habits and bedroom setup that help you sleep well. These tips come from 
the CDC, the National Institutes of Health and research on each habit. No single tip works for everyone. 
Try one or two changes at a time and give each one at least two weeks.
The core principles
Be regular: same bedtime and wake time every day.
Let light and dark set your body clock: bright light by day, dim light at night.
Keep the bed for sleep: the bedroom should be cool, dark, quiet and calm.
Watch what goes in: caffeine, alcohol, nicotine and heavy meals all affect sleep.
Move by day, wind down at night: daytime activity builds healthy sleep pressure; a calm routine signals 
bedtime.
1. Keep a steady schedule
Photo: Ales Krivec, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.
Your body clock likes routine
 Go to bed and get up at about the same time every day, 
even on weekends. Your wake-up time is the most 
important anchor.
 Choose a wake time first, then count back 7 to 9 hours to set 
your bedtime.
 Sleeping in on weekends to "catch up" can shift your body 
clock and make Sunday night harder.
 Go to bed when you feel sleepy, not just tired.
2. Use light to set your body clock
Your circadian rhythm is your internal 24-hour clock. Light is its strongest signal. Light in the morning 
moves the clock earlier and helps you feel alert. Bright light in the evening pushes it later and lowers 
melatonin, the hormone that tells your body it is night.
Bright days, dark nights
 Open the curtains or step outside within an hour of waking. 
Even a cloudy day is much brighter than indoor light.
 Spend time outdoors during the day when you can.
 Dim the lights in your home 1 to 2 hours before bed.
 If you get up at night, use a small night light rather than 
turning on bright overhead lights.

Commons.

3. Make your bedroom a place for sleep
Photo: Quin Stevenson, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.
Cool, dark and quiet
 Cool: a slightly cool room helps the body's temperature 
drop, which helps sleep start.
 Dark: use blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Cover or turn 
away glowing clocks and chargers.
 Quiet: try earplugs, a fan or a white-noise machine.
 Comfortable: a supportive mattress and pillows that fit your 
joints (see the arthritis tips section).
 Use the bed for sleep and intimacy only, not for work, TV or 
worrying.
4. Caffeine, alcohol and nicotine
Photo: Julius Schorzman, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia 
Commons.
Caffeine
 Caffeine blocks adenosine, a brain chemical that builds up 
while you are awake and makes you sleepy.
 In one study, a large dose of caffeine (400 mg, about the 
amount in 4 cups of brewed coffee) still disturbed sleep 
when taken 6 hours before bed. The researchers advise 
avoiding large amounts of caffeine for at least 6 hours 
before bedtime.
 A good rule: no caffeine after about noon to 2 PM. 
Remember tea, cola, energy drinks, chocolate and some 
pain relievers contain caffeine.
Alcohol
 Alcohol may help you fall asleep faster, but it breaks up 
sleep in the second half of the night and reduces REM sleep.
 It also relaxes throat muscles and can make snoring and 
sleep apnea worse.
 Avoid alcohol in the hours before bed. Alcohol can also 
interact with some arthritis medicines, such as 
methotrexate.
 Photo: Auctor quaerendae, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia 
Commons.
Nicotine
Nicotine is a stimulant. Smokers tend to take longer to fall asleep, sleep more lightly and may wake with 
cravings. Quitting helps sleep, joints and heart. Your doctor can help with a plan to quit.

5. Meals, fluids and exercise
 Meals: avoid large, heavy or spicy meals within 2 to 3 hours of bedtime. If you are hungry, a light 
snack is fine.
 Fluids: drink plenty during the day, then cut back in the last hour or two before bed so a full bladder 
does not wake you.
 Exercise: regular physical activity helps people fall asleep faster and sleep longer and more deeply. A 
review of many studies found that evening exercise generally does not harm sleep, but hard 
exercise that ends within about an hour of bedtime may.

CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.
Move every day
 Aim for regular movement most days, such as walking, 
swimming, cycling, tai chi or chair exercise.
 Morning or daytime walks outdoors add helpful daylight.
 For joint-friendly ideas, see the RheumNotes© Motion 
Component.
6. Screens and devices
Power down before bed
 Phones, tablets and computers give off bright, blue-rich 
light. In a Harvard study, people who read on a light-
emitting e-reader before bed took longer to fall asleep, 
made less melatonin and were less alert the next morning 
than when they read a printed book.
 Turn off screens at least 30 minutes before bed (the CDC's 
advice); an hour is even better.
 Keep the phone out of reach, or at least face down and 
silenced, so alerts do not wake you.

Commons.
7. Naps
 Short naps can help if you are very tired, but long or late naps can make it harder to sleep at night.
 If you nap, keep it to about 20 to 30 minutes and take it before mid-afternoon.
 If you have insomnia, it is usually best to avoid naps, so that sleep pressure builds for the night.
 Pacing your activities and resting without sleeping can help with fatigue during the day.
8. Build a wind-down routine
Your brain needs a runway to land. Spend the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed doing the same calm 
activities in the same order each night.

Sleep Tips for Arthritis and Rheumatic Disease
Joint pain, stiffness and some medicines create special sleep challenges. These tips are general. Talk with 
your doctor, physical therapist or occupational therapist about what is right for you.
Time your pain relief
 Plan pain relief so that it covers the night. Ask your doctor whether the timing of your pain medicine 
or anti-inflammatory medicine could be adjusted to help you sleep.
 Use heat before bed for stiff joints (a warm shower, bath or heating pad on a timer). Never sleep on 
a heating pad that stays on. Use cold packs for hot, swollen joints, as your care team advises.
 Gentle range-of-motion exercises or stretching in the evening can ease stiffness.
 Keep a sleep and pain diary to share with your doctor (see the template later in this paper).
Position and support your joints
Photo: Amin, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.
Pillows are tools
 Neck: choose a pillow that keeps your neck level with your 
spine, neither too high nor too flat.
 Hips, knees and low back (side sleeping): place a pillow 
between your knees.
 Low back (back sleeping): place a pillow under your knees.
 Shoulders: hug a pillow to support the top arm, and avoid 
lying on a painful shoulder.
 Hands and wrists: a resting splint at night may help; ask 
your occupational therapist.
 Stomach sleeping often strains the neck and back.
A medium-firm mattress is often comfortable for people with back pain. A mattress topper can cushion 
sore hips and shoulders. Bed rails, a raised bed or a firm chair nearby can make it easier to get in and out 
of bed safely.
Medicines and sleep: talk with your doctor
Never change the dose or timing of a medicine on your own. But do ask your doctor or pharmacist 
whether any of your medicines could be affecting your sleep.

When Sleep Needs More Help
Good habits help most people, but they are not always enough. Sleep disorders are common, and most 
can be treated. If you have ongoing sleep problems, talk with your doctor. The CDC suggests keeping a 
sleep diary for a week or two to bring to your visit.
Insomnia and CBT-I: the first-line treatment
Insomnia means trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, along with daytime problems 
such as fatigue, poor focus or low mood. When it happens at least 3 nights a week for at least 3 
months, doctors call it chronic insomnia.
Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a short, structured program, usually 4 to 8 sessions 
with a trained clinician, in a group, by video or through a digital program. It changes the habits and 
thoughts that keep insomnia going.
CBT-I part What it involves
Stimulus control Use the bed only for sleep and intimacy. Go to bed only when sleepy. Get up if you 
cannot sleep. Keep the same wake time every day. This retrains the brain to link bed 
with sleep.
Sleep restriction For a short time, limit time in bed to about the hours you actually sleep, then widen it as 
sleep becomes solid. This builds a strong, steady sleep drive. It should be guided by a 
clinician.
Cognitive therapy Spot and change worries and beliefs about sleep, such as "If I don't sleep 8 hours, 
tomorrow will be ruined."
Relaxation training Slow breathing, muscle relaxation, imagery or meditation to calm the body and mind.
Sleep education Sleep hygiene and how sleep works. Helpful as part of CBT-I, but not enough alone for 
chronic insomnia.
What the guidelines say
American College of Physicians (2016): recommends CBT-I as the first treatment for adults with chronic 
insomnia (strong recommendation). Sleep medicines may be considered, through shared decision-
making, if CBT-I alone does not work.
American Academy of Sleep Medicine (2021): strongly recommends CBT-I for chronic insomnia in adults. 
It advises against using sleep hygiene education alone as the treatment.
CBT-I also helps many people who live with chronic pain or other medical conditions, and its benefits 
tend to last after treatment ends.
Sleep medicines can help some people for a short time, but they can cause morning grogginess, 
memory problems and falls, especially in older adults, and they may interact with other medicines. Use 
them only under a doctor's guidance.

Sleep apnea
Obstructive sleep apnea happens when the airway in the throat closes again and again during sleep. 
Breathing stops or gets shallow for 10 seconds or more, oxygen drops, and the brain wakes up briefly to 
restart breathing, often hundreds of times a night without the person knowing. Untreated sleep apnea 
raises the risk of high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, diabetes and accidents.
A CPAP machine, mask and tubing. Photo: DL5MDA, 
Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.
Warning signs of sleep apnea
 Loud, regular snoring
 Pauses in breathing, choking or gasping during sleep, often 
noticed by a bed partner
 Feeling very sleepy or falling asleep during the day, even 
after a full night in bed
 Morning headaches or a dry mouth
 Waking often to urinate
 Higher risk with extra weight, a large neck, high blood 
pressure and older age
Your doctor may order a sleep study in a sleep lab or a simpler home sleep apnea test. The most 
common treatment is CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure), a bedside machine that gently blows 
air through a mask to keep the airway open. Oral appliances, weight loss, sleeping on your side and 
other treatments may also help.
Restless legs syndrome
Restless legs syndrome (RLS) causes a strong urge to move the legs, often with uncomfortable creeping, 
crawling or aching feelings. It starts or gets worse when resting, is relieved by moving, and is worse in 
the evening or at night, which makes it hard to fall asleep. Low iron can cause or worsen RLS, so your 
doctor may check your iron levels. Some medicines can also make RLS worse.
When to see a doctor about sleep
