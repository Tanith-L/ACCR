A Note From the Author
Every day in my rheumatology practice, I hold up a model of a knee
or sketch a hand on the back of an exam-table sheet. The moment a
patient sees where the pain is coming from, something changes. Fear
gives way to understanding, and understanding makes it much easier
to take part in decisions about treatment, exercise and protecting the
joints for the long run.
That is why I built this Anatomy Component of RheumNotes©. It
starts with the healthy joint: the bones, cartilage, ligaments, tendons,
bursae, muscles and nerves that let us turn our heads, open a jar,
climb stairs or walk on a beach. Then it shows what happens when
pain enters the picture, how far each joint normally moves, and how
osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis,
spondyloarthritis, gout and common soft-tissue problems change
that anatomy.
I have used real anatomical drawings, X-rays and clinical photographs
whenever an openly licensed one was available, and I have credited
every image. Where no real image of an examination maneuver
existed under an open license, I drew a simple diagram and labeled it
clearly as an illustration. Range-of-motion values come from
published references that I checked, and I have not included any
statistic I could not verify.
Please use this paper alongside your own clinician, not instead of
one. Your joints, your history and your goals are unique. My hope is
that these pages help you ask better questions, recognize warning
signs early, and feel more at home in your own body.

Introduction: How to Use This Paper
Arthritis is not one disease. More than one hundred conditions can affect the joints and the tissues
around them, and each one leaves its own signature on the anatomy. Knowing what a healthy joint looks
like is the first step to understanding what has changed, why a joint hurts or stiffens, and how treatment
and exercise can help.
This white paper is organized into nine regional modules, in the order in which a clinician often examines
the body: the neck, the thoracic and lumbar spine with the sacroiliac joints, shoulders, elbows, hands
and wrists, hips, knees, ankles and feet. Every module follows the same six-part pattern:
 a. Normal anatomy: the key structures, with real labeled images and a color-coded structure key that
matches the RheumNotes© Anatomy Chart.
 b. Normal range of motion (ROM): typical adult values in degrees, with the published source for
each value and an everyday task that uses that motion.
 c. How pain affects function: guarding, compensation, weakness and changes in walking.
 d. How arthritis changes the anatomy: osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid and
psoriatic arthritis), axial spondyloarthritis, gout and soft-tissue conditions, with normal and arthritic
X-rays and photographs side by side.
 e. Examination tests and maneuvers: what each test looks for, how it is done and what a positive
result suggests.
 f. Takeaways: plain-language points for patients and families, followed by a short note for clinicians.
A word about the numbers
Normal range of motion varies with age, sex, body type, genetics and the method of measurement. The
values in this paper are commonly used reference values, most of them published by the American
Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) and reproduced in standard goniometry texts. They are a
guide, not a pass-or-fail test. Bracketed numbers such as [1] refer to the Evidence and References
section. Where a published source gives a different value, both are shown.
A word about the images
Every anatomical drawing, X-ray and clinical photograph in this paper is a real image from an openly
licensed or public-domain source, mainly Wikimedia Commons, OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, Gray's
Anatomy (1918), Sobotta's Atlas (1909), BodyParts3D and Servier Medical Art. Each caption gives a short
credit, and the Image Credits appendix lists the full title, author, license and source address. Images were
used at their original, highest available resolution. A few examination maneuvers had no openly licensed
photograph; these are shown as simple drawn diagrams and are always labeled "Illustration (drawn
diagram, not a photograph)."
Important
This paper is for education. It cannot diagnose a problem or tell you which treatment is right for
you. If a joint is suddenly hot, red and swollen, if you have a fever with joint pain, or if you notice
new weakness, numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control, seek medical care promptly.

The Building Blocks: Joint Types and Tissues
Joints are the places where two or more bones meet. They are classified by how much they move.
Fibrous joints (such as the sutures of the skull) barely move. Cartilaginous joints (such as the discs
between vertebrae and the pubic symphysis) allow a little movement. Synovial joints – the shoulders,
elbows, wrists, fingers, hips, knees, ankles and toes – move freely, and they are the joints most often
affected by arthritis.
 A synovial joint: articular cartilage, joint cavity with synovial fluid,
synovial membrane and fibrous capsule. Image: OpenStax, CC BY 3.0,
 via Wikimedia Commons.
 The six types of synovial joint: pivot, hinge, saddle, plane,
condyloid and ball-and-socket. Image: OpenStax, CC BY 3.0,
 via Wikimedia Commons.
The eight tissues on the RheumNotes© Anatomy Chart
Tissue
What it does, and how arthritis affects it
■ Bones
The rigid framework. Bone ends are covered with cartilage; the bone just beneath (subchondral
bone) thickens or forms cysts in osteoarthritis and can be eroded by inflammation or gout.
■ Joints
Where bones meet. A synovial joint is enclosed by a capsule lined with synovium, which makes the
lubricating synovial fluid. Inflammation of this lining (synovitis) is the core problem in rheumatoid
and psoriatic arthritis.
■ Cartilage
Smooth hyaline cartilage covers bone ends; tougher fibrocartilage forms the menisci of the knee,
the labrum of the shoulder and hip, the TFCC of the wrist and the intervertebral discs. Cartilage has
no nerves or blood vessels and heals poorly.
■ Ligaments
Strong bands that link bone to bone and stabilize joints. Sprains are ligament injuries; inflammation
where ligaments attach to bone (enthesitis) is typical of spondyloarthritis.
■ Tendons
Cords that connect muscle to bone and move joints. Many run in lubricated sheaths. Tendinopathy,
tenosynovitis and ruptures are common in both overuse and inflammatory arthritis.

Tissue
What it does, and how arthritis affects it
■ Bursae
Small, slippery fluid-filled sacs that cushion tendons, muscles and skin as they glide over bone. They
can become inflamed by pressure, overuse, gout, rheumatoid arthritis or infection.
■ Muscles
Muscles move and protect joints. Pain quickly inhibits nearby muscles, and disuse leads to
weakness, which further loads the joint. Strengthening is a cornerstone of treatment.
■ Nerves
Nerves carry movement signals to muscles and sensation back to the brain. Swollen joints, tendons
or bone spurs can compress nerves, causing numbness, tingling or weakness (for example, carpal
tunnel syndrome).
Normal, osteoarthritic and rheumatoid joints
The three medical illustrations below compare a healthy synovial joint with the two most common
patterns of arthritis. In osteoarthritis, cartilage thins and frays, the joint space narrows, and bone spurs
(osteophytes) grow at the joint margins. In rheumatoid arthritis, the synovium becomes inflamed and
thickened (pannus), invading and eroding cartilage and bone. These two patterns recur in every module
that follows.

Pain, guarding and the cycle of disuse
When a joint hurts, the nervous system protects it. Nearby muscles tighten (guarding), the joint is held in
the position of least pressure – usually slightly bent – and we shift work to other joints (compensation).
In the short term, this is useful. Over weeks, however, guarding causes stiffness and contractures,
inhibited muscles weaken, and neighboring joints become overloaded. Walking changes too: steps
shorten, the painful leg spends less time on the ground (an antalgic gait), and balance may suffer. Each
module describes these patterns for that region, because recognizing them is the first step to reversing
them with treatment, exercise and therapy.

Module 1: Neck (Cervical Spine)
The neck is a stack of seven small vertebrae (C1 to C7) that carry the weight of the head and let us
look up, down and around. It also protects the spinal cord and the nerve roots that travel to the
shoulders, arms and hands.
a. Normal anatomy
Each pair of vertebrae is linked at three points: a cushioning disc in front and two small facet joints
behind. Between C3 and C7 there are also small uncovertebral joints at the edges of the vertebral
bodies. Strong ligaments run the length of the spine, and layers of muscles hold the head balanced over
the shoulders.
The upper two vertebrae are special. The atlas (C1) cradles the skull and allows much of the nodding
motion. The axis (C2) has a peg, the dens, around which the atlas turns; a large share of neck rotation
happens here. Nerve roots leave through small openings called foramina; when these narrow, arm pain,
numbness or weakness can result.

 Guarding: neck muscles tighten to splint a painful joint, causing a stiff, "locked" neck and headaches
at the base of the skull.
 Compensation: people turn the whole trunk instead of the head (for example, when reversing a car),
shifting strain to the upper back and shoulders.
 Posture: a forward-head posture often develops, increasing the work of the neck and upper-back
muscles.
 Weakness and nerve symptoms: a pinched nerve root can send pain, tingling or weakness into the
shoulder blade, arm or hand; grip and fine hand tasks suffer.
 Balance and gait: a stiff neck limits scanning of the surroundings; rarely, spinal cord compression
(myelopathy) causes clumsy hands and an unsteady walk, which needs prompt evaluation.
d. How arthritis changes the anatomy
 Osteoarthritis / cervical spondylosis. Discs lose height, facet and uncovertebral joints develop
osteophytes (bone spurs), and joint spaces narrow. Spurs can narrow the foramina or the spinal
canal. [23]
 Rheumatoid arthritis. Inflamed synovium around the atlas and dens can loosen the transverse
ligament, allowing atlanto-axial instability, one reason neck X-rays may be requested before
anesthesia in long-standing RA. [24]
 Axial spondyloarthritis (including ankylosing spondylitis). Inflammation at ligament attachments
leads to new bone, bridging between vertebrae and loss of neck motion; a rigid neck is more
vulnerable to fracture after falls. [26]
 Psoriatic arthritis. Can involve the neck facet joints and cause stiffness similar to axial
spondyloarthritis. [25]
 Soft-tissue and nerve conditions. Muscle strain, myofascial pain and pinched nerve roots
(radiculopathy) often accompany arthritis.

f. Takeaways
For patients and families
 Your neck is built for motion; gentle, regular movement usually helps more than holding it still.
 Pain that travels into the arm, or numbness, tingling or weakness in the hand, should be
checked by your clinician.
 Clumsy hands, trouble with buttons, or a new unsteady walk need prompt medical attention.
 If you have long-standing rheumatoid arthritis, tell any surgical or anesthesia team; your neck
may need special care.
Clinician note
StatPearls notes that the Spurling test is considered highly specific with only mild-to-moderate sensitivity [6]; a
negative test does not exclude radiculopathy. In long-standing RA, consider flexion-extension radiographs for
atlanto-axial instability when symptoms or anesthesia warrant. Screen for myelopathy (Hoffmann sign, gait, hand
dexterity) in cervical spondylosis.

Module 2: Spine (Thoracic, Lumbar and Sacroiliac)
Below the neck, 12 thoracic vertebrae anchor the ribs, 5 lumbar vertebrae carry most of the body's
weight, and the sacrum joins the pelvis at the two sacroiliac (SI) joints. Together they let us bend,
twist, lift and stand tall.
a. Normal anatomy
The spine has gentle curves: the thoracic spine curves backward (kyphosis) and the lumbar spine curves
forward (lordosis). These curves act like a spring, absorbing load. Each disc has a tough outer ring
(annulus fibrosus) and a gel-like center (nucleus pulposus). Paired facet joints guide motion: the
thoracic spine favors rotation, while the lumbar spine favors bending forward and back.
The sacroiliac joints link the spine to the pelvis. They are strong, tightly bound by ligaments and move
only a small amount, but they transfer the load of the upper body to the legs with every step. The SI
joints are a key site of inflammation in axial spondyloarthritis.

Module 3: Shoulders
The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body. A large ball (the head of the humerus) sits on a
shallow socket (the glenoid), so it depends on muscles, tendons and ligaments – especially the
rotator cuff – for stability.
a. Normal anatomy
The shoulder is really several joints working together. The glenohumeral joint gives most of the motion,
while the scapula glides on the rib cage to position the socket. A rim of cartilage, the labrum, deepens
the socket. Above the joint, the acromion forms a roof; between that roof and the rotator cuff lies the
subacromial bursa, a slippery sac that lets the tendons glide.
The four rotator cuff muscles start on the scapula and wrap around the humeral head to hold it centered
while the larger deltoid lifts the arm. When the cuff is weak or torn, the ball rides upward and pinches
the tendons and bursa beneath the acromion.

Module 4: Elbows
The elbow is a hinge that bends and straightens the arm, combined with a pivot that turns the palm
up and down. It positions the hand for eating, grooming, lifting and work.
a. Normal anatomy
Three bones meet at the elbow inside one joint capsule. The humeroulnar joint works as a hinge; the
radial head spins within the annular ligament to turn the palm. Strong collateral ligaments on each side
keep the hinge stable.
The bony bumps on each side, the epicondyles, are anchor points: the wrist and finger extensor muscles
start on the outer (lateral) side and the flexors on the inner (medial) side. The ulnar nerve runs in a
groove behind the medial epicondyle – the "funny bone." Over the point of the elbow, the olecranon
bursa cushions the skin.

Module 5: Hands and Wrists
The hand and wrist contain 27 bones and dozens of small joints, tendons and ligaments packed into
a small space. They allow both power grip and fine precision – and they are where many forms of
arthritis show their earliest signs.
a. Normal anatomy
The wrist is a two-row arrangement of eight carpal bones sitting on the end of the radius. On the
little-finger side, the TFCC cushions the gap between the ulna and the carpus. The palm-side arch of
carpal bones is roofed by the transverse carpal ligament, forming the carpal tunnel, through which nine
flexor tendons and the median nerve pass.
Each finger has three joints – the MCP (knuckle), PIP (middle) and DIP (end) joints; the thumb has a
highly mobile CMC joint at its base. Long tendons from the forearm bend (flexors) and straighten
(extensors) the fingers, gliding in lubricated tendon sheaths, while small intrinsic muscles in the hand
fine-tune movement.

Module 6: Hips
The hip is a deep ball-and-socket joint that bears up to several times body weight with each step. It
trades some motion for stability, and it is central to walking, climbing stairs, sitting and getting up.
a. Normal anatomy
The rounded femoral head fits deeply into the cup-shaped acetabulum, which is deepened further by
the fibrocartilage labrum. Very strong ligaments – especially the iliofemoral (Y) ligament, one of the
strongest in the body – wrap the capsule and limit over-extension.
The gluteus medius and minimus attach to the greater trochanter and keep the pelvis level when
standing on one leg. The iliopsoas is the main hip flexor. Bursae over the trochanter and in front of the
joint reduce friction. Hip-joint pain is usually felt in the groin and may travel to the knee, while pain on
the outer hip usually comes from the tendons and bursa.

Module 7: Knees
The knee is the largest joint in the body. It is mainly a hinge, but it also glides and rotates slightly,
and it relies heavily on ligaments, menisci and the thigh muscles for stability.
a. Normal anatomy
The rounded femoral condyles rest on the flatter tibial plateau. Two C-shaped menisci deepen the fit
and spread load. The cruciate ligaments cross inside the joint to prevent the tibia sliding forward (ACL)
or backward (PCL), while the collateral ligaments on each side resist side-to-side stress.
The patella sits within the quadriceps tendon and glides in a groove on the femur, increasing the
leverage of the quadriceps. Several bursae surround the knee; the suprapatellar pouch connects with
the joint, so a knee effusion often bulges above the kneecap. A Baker (popliteal) cyst forms behind the
knee when joint fluid collects in the bursa between the semimembranosus and gastrocnemius.

Module 8: Ankles
The ankle connects the leg to the foot. The main ankle joint lifts and lowers the foot, while the
subtalar joint just below it tilts the foot in and out to adapt to uneven ground.
a. Normal anatomy
The talus fits into a mortise formed by the tibia and fibula – the talocrural joint – which mainly bends the
foot up (dorsiflexion) and down (plantarflexion). Below it, the subtalar joint between talus and
calcaneus allows the foot to roll in (inversion) and out (eversion).
The outer ankle is supported by three lateral ligaments; the anterior talofibular ligament is the one
most often sprained. The broad deltoid ligament supports the inner side. The Achilles tendon – the
strongest tendon in the body – attaches the calf muscles to the heel, cushioned by the retrocalcaneal
bursa. Behind the inner ankle, the tibialis posterior tendon supports the arch.

Module 9: Feet
Each foot has 26 bones and more than 30 joints arranged in arches that absorb shock and then
become a rigid lever for push-off. The forefoot is a very common early site of inflammatory arthritis
and gout.
a. Normal anatomy
The foot is divided into the hindfoot (talus, calcaneus), midfoot (navicular, cuboid, cuneiforms) and
forefoot (metatarsals, toes). The medial longitudinal arch is supported by bones, the spring ligament,
the tibialis posterior tendon and the plantar fascia, which runs from the heel to the toes.
When the big toe bends upward during push-off, the plantar fascia tightens like a cable around a winch,
raising the arch and stiffening the foot – the windlass mechanism. The MTP joints take great load in
walking; small interdigital nerves run between the metatarsal heads, where they can become irritated
(Morton neuroma).

c. How pain affects function
 Forefoot pain: people shift weight to the heel and outer foot and shorten their stride; walking feels
like "walking on pebbles."
 Stiff big toe: loss of 1st MTP extension forces the foot to roll outward and reduces push-off.
 Heel pain: first steps in the morning hurt most with plantar fasciitis.
 Shoes and balance: deformities make shoes painful; reduced activity and altered gait raise the risk of
falls.
d. How arthritis changes the anatomy
 Rheumatoid arthritis. MTP synovitis is often an early sign. Over time, erosions of the metatarsal
heads, hallux valgus, clawed toes and forefoot fat-pad displacement cause painful calluses under the
metatarsal heads. [24, 22]
 Osteoarthritis. Common at the 1st MTP (hallux rigidus), with dorsal osteophytes limiting toe
extension, and at midfoot joints. [23]
 Gout. The 1st MTP is the classic site of an acute attack (podagra). Chronic gout causes tophi and
"punched-out" erosions with overhanging edges. [27]
 Psoriatic arthritis. Dactylitis (whole-toe "sausage" swelling), DIP involvement and enthesitis at the
heel. [25]
 Plantar fasciitis, Morton neuroma and bursitis. Heel pain at the plantar fascia origin; interdigital
nerve irritation between the 3rd and 4th toes; intermetatarsal bursitis in RA. [29, 22]

Glossary
Term
Meaning
Ankylosis
Fusion of a joint or spinal segment so that it can no longer move.
Articular (hyaline) cartilage
The smooth, glossy tissue covering bone ends inside a synovial joint.
Boutonnière deformity
Finger with the middle (PIP) joint bent and the end (DIP) joint bent backward.
Bursa / bursitis
A small fluid-filled sac that reduces friction; bursitis is its inflammation.
Crepitus
A grinding or crackling felt or heard with joint motion.
Dactylitis
Swelling of a whole finger or toe ("sausage digit"), typical of psoriatic arthritis.
Effusion
Excess fluid inside a joint.
Enthesis / enthesitis
The site where a tendon or ligament attaches to bone; enthesitis is its inflammation.
Erosion
An area where bone has been worn away, typically by inflammatory synovium or
gout.
Fibrocartilage
Tough, fibrous cartilage found in menisci, discs and the labrum.
Goniometer
A protractor-like tool used to measure joint angles.
Guarding
Involuntary muscle tightening to protect a painful area.
Heberden / Bouchard nodes
Bony enlargements of the end (DIP) and middle (PIP) finger joints in osteoarthritis.
Ligament
A strong band connecting bone to bone.
Meniscus / labrum
Fibrocartilage rims that deepen and cushion the knee (meniscus) or the shoulder and
hip sockets (labrum).
Osteophyte
A bone spur growing at the edge of a joint, typical of osteoarthritis.
Radiculopathy
Pain, numbness or weakness caused by a pinched spinal nerve root.
Range of motion (ROM)
How far a joint can move in each direction, measured in degrees.
Sacroiliitis
Inflammation of the sacroiliac joints, a hallmark of axial spondyloarthritis.
Subchondral sclerosis / cyst
Thickened bone or small fluid-filled cavities beneath damaged cartilage.
Swan-neck deformity
Finger with the middle (PIP) joint bent backward and the end (DIP) joint bent.
Syndesmophyte
A thin bony bridge between vertebrae in axial spondyloarthritis.
Synovial fluid
The slippery fluid that lubricates and nourishes a joint.
Synovium / synovitis
The thin lining of a joint capsule; synovitis is its inflammation.
Tendon / tendinopathy
A cord linking muscle to bone; tendinopathy is pain and degeneration of a tendon.
Tophus (tophi)
A deposit of uric acid crystals in or around joints in chronic gout.
Trendelenburg gait
A gait in which the pelvis drops on the side of the swinging leg because of hip
abductor weakness.
Ulnar deviation
Drifting of the fingers toward the little-finger side, typical of long-standing RA.
